# 15. Techno-economic assessment

The model is a live-formula workbook: `assets/cost_model.xlsx`, generated by
`assets/build_cost_model.py`. Yellow cells are editable assumptions with a stated
basis; blue cells are formulas. Nothing below should be trusted further than its
inputs.

## 15.1 Base case

Using the proposed starting specification (120 g·m⁻² paper, 10 g·m⁻² coat weight,
25 % w/w complex in the coating, 0.02 m² insert, 85 % yield, 1.35× overhead):

| Output | Value |
|---|---|
| Complex in coating | 2.50 g·m⁻² |
| Subtotal before yield | $0.704 /m² |
| Yield-adjusted | $0.828 /m² |
| Materials + conversion per insert | $0.0166 |
| Fully loaded (incl. overwrap, QC) | $0.0416 |
| **With overhead and margin** | **$0.056 per insert** |
| **Cost per kg produce protected** | **$0.011 /kg** |
| 1-MCP delivered per insert | ~1.5 mg |

The delivered payload of ~1.5 mg falls inside the 0.5–5 mg range proposed in
Section 9.2, so the cost case and the dose case are at least mutually consistent
— which is a check, not a validation.

## 15.2 Cost structure

At base-case assumptions the **1-MCP/α-CD complex is ~36 % of pre-yield cost**
($0.300 of $0.704 per m²), with coating and drying next at ~21 %. Paper is
almost negligible (~2 %).

This is the important structural finding: **the active dominates, and the
substrate does not.** Two consequences:

* Optimising the paper is not worth effort. Choosing the cheapest coatable stock
  is fine.
* Every gram of coat weight is a direct cost, so the coat-weight optimisation in
  Section 11 is simultaneously a *release-control* and a *cost* exercise. If a
  lower coat weight gives the same delivered dose, it is doubly valuable.

## 15.3 What the model deliberately does not claim

**No competitor price is asserted.** Published pricing for SmartFresh,
HarvestHold Fresh, Vidre+ and Hazel 100 could not be retrieved. Inventing a
benchmark to declare this product "cheaper" would be a fabrication and would
invalidate the comparison. The benchmark cell is left at zero for the reader to
fill with a real quote.

So the honest statement is: **this model produces a defensible internal cost, and
no verified comparison to incumbents.** The comparison is an open question
(Section 19).

**Registration and compliance costs are excluded from the per-unit model.** For a
FIFRA-regulated product these are potentially larger than manufacturing cost and
behave as a programme cost, not a unit cost. A registration requirement could
dominate the entire economic picture (Section 14), and no credible figure can be
put on it without specialist advice.

## 15.4 Sensitivity

| Driver | Low | Base | High | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Complex price ($/kg)** | 40 | **120** | 400 | **The dominant uncertainty.** Unverified; a supplier quote is the highest-value single data point in this model |
| Coat weight (g·m⁻²) | 4 | **10** | 20 | Scales complex usage linearly; also the release lever |
| Complex fraction (w/w) | 0.10 | **0.25** | 0.40 | Scales complex usage linearly |
| Insert area (m²) | 0.01 | **0.02** | 0.04 | Scales material per package |
| Coating + drying ($/m²) | 0.05 | **0.15** | 0.50 | Falls sharply with volume; dominant at pilot scale |
| Yield | 0.70 | **0.85** | 0.95 | Waste of a speciality active is expensive |

Because complex price, coat weight and complex fraction all enter linearly and
multiply, the plausible range on cost per insert is roughly an order of magnitude
— **from under $0.02 to over $0.30**. That spread is too wide to support any
commercial decision, and narrowing it does not require lab work: it requires **a
supplier quotation and an assay of the complex's actual 1-MCP content.** Both are
Phase 1 actions.

## 15.5 Are chitosan and CNC needed at commercial scale?

No — and the cost model shows why the question is moot rather than marginal.
Since the active dominates cost, the argument against chitosan and CNC is not
primarily economic; it is that they **add water, hygroscopicity and process steps**
(Section 7) while contributing nothing the design needs. Their cost is a
secondary reason to omit them.

Of the cheaper alternatives the brief lists, the binder screen (Section 10.4)
covers the relevant span: shellac/ethanolic (water-free, protects the payload),
PEG (literature-anchored for this exact purpose), and water-based latex/HPMC
(cheapest, highest retention risk). Starch and alginate are aqueous and
hygroscopic — the same objection as chitosan. Commercial water-based coatings are
worth screening precisely because they are cheapest and most industrially
standard, but they must be measured for retention, not assumed adequate.

## 15.6 Scale

Minimum viable volume is set by **coating-line economics, not materials**. A
slot-die or gravure line has a minimum practical run length; below it, setup and
cleaning dominate and the $0.15/m² conversion assumption is optimistic by an
order of magnitude. For early work, toll-coating on an existing line is far more
sensible than dedicated equipment — which also means the design must fit
*someone else's* line, reinforcing the Section 10 recommendation to use a lab
method (Meyer rod) with a real industrial analogue.
