Scope of this analysis
Sources
Tariffs are sourced directly from public statements from The White House in 2025.
Import values by commodity & country are sourced from the US Census Bureau.
Personal Consumption Expenditure is sourced from the Bureau of Economic Analysis.
Methodology
Average tariff by country includes commodity specific rates and exemptions. They are 
weighted by 2024 imports by country & commodity.
Tariffs are then grouped into Autos, Energy, and All Other.
Imports are scaled by the tariff rate and weighted against Personal Consumption 
Expenditures to get impact on inflation.

Durable goods face near immediate impacts from tariffs while wage inflation follows 
goods inflation

Key Assumptions:
• The cost associated with tariffs will be passed through to the consumer.
• Baseline inflation is 2.8% based on Personal Consumption Expenditures Price Index.
• There is 20.3T in consumption which is ~$13.9T services (referred to as “Services Segments”) and ~$6.3T goods (referred to as “Goods Segments”).
• The $13.9T in services comprises of 55% labor and 45% services.
• Tariffs are assumed to have initial value of 2.5%. Motor will increase to 19.9%, Energy to 7.3% and all other to 21.4%.
• Tariffs will be passed through the cost of goods sold to the consumer but, companies will not load their profits for tariffs.

Loss costs will rise depending on LOB and tariff scenario
This increase is presented as a one-time change, but will be realized over a period of 2 to 4 years
How to use these figures
• Start with your pre-tariff view of inflation, trend historical loss to today’s dollars, then scale by (1 + Tariff Effect %)
Further Considerations
• The range of estimates provided reflects sensitivity to assumptions on reshoring and cost passed through to consumers
i. Reshoring: “Gross” is based on import levels as of today, while “Net” assumes there will be reshoring due to the tariffs.
ii. Pass Through: “Full” scales both profit margin and costs for tariffs. “Partial” ~70% scales only costs while holding profit dollars constant, 
which is similar to an estimate made by Goldman Sachs. Goldman estimates American businesses are currently absorbing 60% of tariffs.
• This estimate assumes that producer and consumer behavior does not change, that imports are consistent and simply scaled 
up by the tariff rate. It does not consider secondary effects of tariffs such as policy uncertainty, price impact on domestic 
goods, possible recession impact on demand, or the scarcity of goods due to a disruption in global trade.
• Tariffs are treated as a shock and the initial goods price effect is 3.5%. The final total tariff price effect of 5.0% is likely to 
materialize over a period of two or more years as wages react to tariff inflation in arrears.

Reinsurers Are Not Materially Affected by Recent Market Volatility
Executive Summary
• A series of unexpectedly severe tariff announcements from the new U.S. administration in early April 2025 triggered an 
episode of significant capital market volatility.
○ On April 8, the VIX Index exceeded 50 for only the third time in the last 35 years
○ Very unusually, U.S. equities, U.S. treasuries and the U.S. dollar all sold-off at the same time
• The April 9 suspension of most ‘reciprocal’ tariffs for 90-days has since restored a degree of stability
• Investors fear tariffs and the trade war with China will result in stagflation and potentially recession
• Re/insurers are not immune to macro headwinds, but most other sectors are more exposed 
○ In most cases, investment in shares is low relative to capital on the balance sheet, particularly in the P&C market
○ Leverage to bonds tends to be much higher, and credit quality is generally good.  Interest rate changes or bond spreads 
could impact capital positions on the back of unrealized losses
• In terms of share prices, reinsurers are out-performing in the current environment 
Key Takeaways
• Market volatility to-date is not expected to have a material effect on reinsurers’ results or balance sheet strength.
Source:   Aon Market Analysis 18

Proprietary 22
US Court of International Trade (CIT) ruled against broad-based tariffs
May 29:  All tariffs still in effect while the Trump administration appeals this decision
Federal court rules that tariffs implemented under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) are unlawful
Two separate lawsuits were brought before the court, a group of small businesses and 12 U.S. states
Ruling covers ‘worldwide, retaliatory tariffs’ (10% reciprocal) & ‘trafficking tariffs’ (20% China, 25% Canada & Mexico)
1) Worldwide & Retaliatory Tariff Orders exceed the authority given to the President by IEEPA. The President can regulate 
imports, but not by using tariffs. Tariffs are beyond the powers granted by the law since they are ‘unbounded’.
2) Trafficking tariffs were found to be invalid as they did not address the threats outlined in the orders.
The Plaintiffs don’t have to pay the tariffs, but how does this apply to the rest of the country?
Duties, imposts, and excises must be applied to all states, otherwise they do not meet the constitutional requirement for 
uniformity (Article I, Sec 8, Clause 1). If 12 states do not have to pay the tariffs, then no states must pay the tariffs.
The Justice Department has said the lawsuits should be dismissed
1) The plaintiffs have not been harmed by tariffs that they have not yet paid, and
2) Only Congress, not private businesses, can challenge a national emergency declared by the president under IEEPA
What happens next?
Jul 31, oral arguments made to US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. It could go all the way to the Supreme Court.
Sources:  Cases 25-00066 & 25-00077, US court blocks most Trump tariffs, says president exceeded his authority | Reuters

What does the CIT ruling mean for the insurance industry?
Auto & Commercial Property are still impacted
Uncertainty itself contributes to price volatility
Executive Orders have not been reversed, so IEEPA tariffs are still being collected while the court ruling is appealed.
Businesses are back in a holding pattern on strategy, hiring, expansion, etc.
If the ruling stands, there could be a rush of orders from importers, leading to a spike in shipping costs and delays at ports.
Tariffs remain on cars, steel and aluminum
These tariffs were applied under the authority of Sections 232 & 301 of congressional acts.
Section 232 relates to Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (document, summary). Section 301 relates to Trade Act of 1974 (link).
Tariffs justified by Sections 232 & 301 could have more staying power than IEEPA justified tariffs
President Trump implemented steel & aluminum tariffs during his first term which survived multiple legal challenges.
These sector-specific tariffs were supported by an investigation done by the Secretary of Commerce.
The President could continue with tariffs in this manner, but an investigation for each sector would slow the pace of tariffs.
Other avenues for tariffs
The Republican party still maintains a narrow margin in both the House and Senate. They could pass legislation on the matter.
Similar to IEEPA, cite other authorities that haven’t been used for tariffs, such as Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (up to 
15% for 150 days, unless extended by Congress), or Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (Depression-era law, up to 50%).

Tariff Implications for P&C insurers
1) Insurance costs will rise
i. Supply chains take several years to move countries. In the short term, the cost of tariffs will be paid by consumers 
importing goods such as vehicles or building materials.
ii. As seen during Covid, disruption in the international supply chain can lead to cost increases for domestic goods.
iii. Some goods might become unavailable at any price if countries apply export restrictions or sanctions. Shortage of 
critical building materials could leave some production stalled or delayed, like the Auto industry saw during Covid.
• April 4:  China has issued restrictions on exporting rare earth materials to US and other countries (Link).
2) Profitability will suffer until rate increases can be filed and earned into premium
i. When Covid sparked inflation in 2020, it took insurers 2 years to recognize the cost increase and then earn the rate 
increases into their premium.
ii. It may only take 1-year this time, since the cost increase was announced via an Executive Order.
3) Assets may be stressed if global economies slow
i. Equity & bond markets experienced a lot of volatility following tariff announcements.
ii. Investment income could decrease if equity and bond markets deteriorate, thus impacting operating income.
4) Reciprocal tariffs from other countries could depress economic activity, thus decreasing premium volume
i. From April 4 to April 10, the US and China exchanged retaliatory tariffs. US tariff on China is 145%, and China has a 
125% on the US. The EU announced a 20% retaliatory tariff but then paused it when the US announced a pause.
ii. May 12:  China reset to 10% reciprocal, other tariffs remain in place. This deal has been extended to November 9.

How much of a tariff will be passed through to the cost of goods?
A full pass through assumes a 10% tariff yields 10% price increase
The 10% price increase for a 10% tariff is a constant 
(percentage) margin price model. In the table to the left we 
see that this model would yield a higher profit post tariff than 
pre-tariff for a product with a 40% margin.
It is likely that some part of a tariff is absorbed by the seller. 
In the constant profit scenario with a 40% margin, the tariff 
itself is fully passed through to the buyer, but the price 
increase is 6% not 10%. Actual margins vary by product 
category. If the margin is 0%, then the price increase to 
maintain the 0% margin is the same as the tariff increase.
The actual level of tariff passed through depends upon 
elasticity of supply and demand as sellers seek to maximize 
profit and buyers optimize consumption.
Goldman Sachs estimates that American businesses are 
currently absorbing around 60% of tariffs - The Economist
Their initial assumption was that 70% of tariffs would be 
passed onto consumers.  Goldman Sachs
1) No Pass-through:  Companies absorb the cost of the tariff 
to keep prices constant. Both profit & margin decrease.
2) Constant profit:  Companies increase prices to keep profit 
the same, but at a lower margin.
3) Constant Margin:  Companies increase prices to keep the 
margin the same, which increases their profits.
4) Shrink-flation: Same price & margin, but fewer units.

How can personal auto insurers address these concerns?
Physical damage has subtle exposure trend built in via the model year and symbol factors
Homeowners, Commercial Property, GL, and Work Comp have explicit exposure trending built into them.
Property has inflation guard. Each year you can see your insured value increase.
General Liability you see inflation in the sales exposure base year-to-year.
Workers’ Compensation has inflation built into payroll.
Personal auto is more subtle as the exposure trending happens within their rating algorithm
1. Model Year/Vehicle Age: A policyholder’s model year does not change but new models are released, and a new factor is 
added to the model year rating table. This will be a 4-5% increase, for example, to recognize the rise in costs year-to-year.
2. Symbols: Auto physical damage is rated with what are called “symbols”, this factor is meant to address the physical and 
cost characteristics similarities between vehicles. In practice, if new vehicles are 8% more expensive due to tariffs, they 
will receive a higher symbol and thus a higher premium.
Exposure trends in personal auto exist and will help mitigate effects of tariffs without filing rate change, specifically on new cars. 
Strategy will need to vary by state due to some DOIs being more permissive on rating structure and rate changes than others.

Presidential Action to avoid stacking of some tariffs
April 29 Executive Order 14289 is very impactful for auto manufacturers
Addressing Certain Tariffs on Imported Articles – The White House
Presidential Actions on tariffs state that they are “in addition to any other duties, fees, exactions, and charges.”
Some commodities are covered by multiple actions and would therefore receive multiple tariffs stacked on top of each other.
• An imported aluminum car door would have faced a 50% tariff, 25% Aluminum + 25% Car Parts. Now its only 25%.
The new Executive Order covers the following tariffs in order of priority:
a) Cars and Car Parts, Proclamation 10908
i. Cars & Car Parts are not subject to Canada & Mexico good-specific tariffs or Steel/Aluminum tariffs listed below
b) Specific goods from Canada, Executive Orders 14193, 14197, 14226, 14231
c) Specific goods from Mexico, Executive Orders 14194, 14198, 14227, 14232
ii. Goods subject to specific tariffs on Canada & Mexico are not subject to Steel & Aluminum tariffs
d) Aluminum, Proclamations 9704, 9980, 10895
iii. Goods subject to aluminum tariffs are not subject to steel tariffs
e) Steel, Proclamations 9705, 9980, 10896
Tariffs listed in this Executive Order can still stack with tariffs not listed here, such as reciprocal or fentanyl tariffs – Sec. 3 (c)

Presidential Action to alleviate tariffs on imported car parts
Proclamation 10925 may reduce car part tariff impact by ~24% in year 1, less in year 2
Amendments to Adjusting Imports of Automobiles and Automobile Parts Into the United States – The White House
Tariffs on car parts will now have an offset credit based on total value of cars assembled in the US
Tariff offset on imported parts up to 15% of the 25% tariff in US in Year 1. Up to a 10% credit in Year 2. None after that.
Year 1 Max Offset = 3.75% of aggregate MSRP from all cars assembled in the US
 April 3, 2025 to April 30, 2026; 3.75% offset = 25% Tariff * 15%
Year 2 Max Offset = 2.5% of aggregate MSRP from all cars assembled in the US
 May 1, 2026 to April 30, 2027; 2.5% offset = 25% Tariff * 10%
Only offsets tariffs from Proclamation 10908 (cars & car parts), capped the total amount of tariffs paid under that Proclamation
Import content and final assembly country sourced from data required by the American Automobile Labeling Act
For each model assembled in US, we scale it by the sales volume for that car and average transaction price to get total MSRP
Industry Aggregate MSRP * 65% US Assembly * 3.75% Offset = 2.4% point reduction in the effective tariff on new cars
This creates a carrying cost for auto manufacturers who must pay tariffs in the meantime, then apply for an offset a year later.
