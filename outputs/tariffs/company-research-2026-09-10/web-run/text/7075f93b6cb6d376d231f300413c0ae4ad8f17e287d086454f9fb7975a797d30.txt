Many of you know I'm vehemently opposed to how POTUS is developing tariff policy, with the proposed auto tariffs (both on finished vehicles and especially auto parts) being one of the most misguided policies I could have imagined. Instead, let me propose a sensible set of policies that would almost assuredly INCREASE the production of vehicles in North America and, in doing so, help U.S. workers in the auto sector. This policy is informed by recent theory on efficient use of tariffs (see https://lnkd.in/gbrfdngf). It consists of the following steps: [1]: Apply tariffs ONLY to finished vehicles assembled outside of North America (e.g., Japan, South Korea, Germany, and the UK). The reason is that vehicles assembled in these countries include very little North American content (and hence are a direct competitor to U.S. value added). This will make vehicles imported outside of North America less attractive for U.S. consumers. [2]: DON'T apply tariffs to imported auto parts from any country. The reason is simple: tariffing those imported parts raises the cost of domestically assembled vehicles. Likewise, DON'T tariff steel or aluminum. Because we aren't tariffing imported inputs, the cost of vehicles assembled in the USA, Mexico, and Canada aren't affected (or minimally so). Therefore, these vehicles are now more competitive relative to imports outside of North America due to Step 1. [3]: The reason we don't tariff Mexican or Canadian assembled vehicles is that they contain a significant amount of U.S. value added. Consequently, even if GM imports a pickup truck from Mexico, American workers still benefited by making some of the parts. We can revisit USMCA rules about how much value must come from the USA and Canada in the renegotiation. Implication: I could actually get behind a strategic, surgical policy of tariffing vehicles assembled outside of North America. My proposed tariffs would have limited inflationary effect on vehicle prices, as dealers of vehicles imported from Asia and Europe would have to contend with North American vehicles having seen no significant increase in cost (hence making it harder to raise prices for fear of losing market share).
