Many of you know I'm vehemently opposed to how POTUS is developing tariff policy, with the proposed auto tariffs (both on finished vehicles and especially auto parts) being one of the most misguided policies I could have imagined. Instead, let me propose a sensible set of policies that would almost assuredly INCREASE the production of vehicles in North America and, in doing so, help U.S. workers in the auto sector. This policy is informed by recent theory on efficient use of tariffs (see https://lnkd.in/gbrfdngf). It consists of the following steps: [1]: Apply tariffs ONLY to finished vehicles assembled outside of North America (e.g., Japan, South Korea, Germany, and the UK). The reason is that vehicles assembled in these countries include very little North American content (and hence are a direct competitor to U.S. value added). This will make vehicles imported outside of North America less attractive for U.S. consumers. [2]: DON'T apply tariffs to imported auto parts from any country. The reason is simple: tariffing those imported parts raises the cost of domestically assembled vehicles. Likewise, DON'T tariff steel or aluminum. Because we aren't tariffing imported inputs, the cost of vehicles assembled in the USA, Mexico, and Canada aren't affected (or minimally so). Therefore, these vehicles are now more competitive relative to imports outside of North America due to Step 1. [3]: The reason we don't tariff Mexican or Canadian assembled vehicles is that they contain a significant amount of U.S. value added. Consequently, even if GM imports a pickup truck from Mexico, American workers still benefited by making some of the parts. We can revisit USMCA rules about how much value must come from the USA and Canada in the renegotiation. Implication: I could actually get behind a strategic, surgical policy of tariffing vehicles assembled outside of North America. My proposed tariffs would have limited inflationary effect on vehicle prices, as dealers of vehicles imported from Asia and Europe would have to contend with North American vehicles having seen no significant increase in cost (hence making it harder to raise prices for fear of losing market share). As things stand, since everyone's costs are going up come next week, dealers will feel more comfortable raising prices. #supplychain #markets #economics #shipsandshipping #freight #trucking

Most certainly appreciate the insight but a tax by any other name is a tax and the same problems still exist. For example, Germany ships cars here because they are far down the scale curve and are pretty much shipping at the marginal cost of production. Creating a sub-scale plant here (or more than likely Mexico) or significantly expanding existing facilities to accommodate additional platforms would add cost that have to be passed on to consumers or forgone with the implication being lower consumer choice. Further, I cannot imagine any German company building engines in North America - that’s the core capability - not going to happen; again, higher prices or lower choice. Now add on the retaliatory tariffs and the resulting job losses and, well, I know you get the point. The short is there is no free lunch and if we want additional U.S. manufacturing we must be willing to pay the costs and have a smaller economy.

There's a lot to be considered and a lot that is till to be decided. I am in agreement with your 1st point, and most of your 3rd. I think there might be some value in creating a stipulation that a vehicle allowed to enter the US from CA or MX without a tariff should be majoratively built or assembled with manufactured parts or source commodities from the US. With respect to the 2nd point, I think there is still an opportunity for the US to target and negotiate with certain foreign countries to create more parity in trade for parts that are imported into the US, otherwise persuade sourcing of those parts to countries that are better aligned with the trade strategies of the US. With respect to your implication, I am already being quoted "tariff pricing" on vehicles (specifically material handling) that our organization is purchasing in April. I think a lot of what we are seeing is vendors capitalizing on the opportunity to price gouge based on the geopolitical and economic new cycle.

    BUSINESS Tariffs take a bite out of U.S. auto sector The U.S. wants to turn the North American auto industry into just the American auto industry — but it might cripple the whole sector in the process. What happened: U.S. automakers have likely paid around US$10.6 billion in tariffs on parts made in Canada and Mexico so far this year, a new analysis found. That hefty sum is just slightly less than the combined net income of Ford and GM last year. The true cost of tariffs to automakers is likely higher, as the calculation doesn’t account for separate tariffs on aluminum or steel or imports from Europe and Asia. Why it’s happening: Charging carmakers to import parts from Canada and Mexico is part of the Trump administration’s grand plan to do away with North America’s decades-old integrated auto sector and bring the whole supply chain inside U.S. borders. To some extent, that plan is working — automakers like Stellantis are cancelling production in Canada and moving it to the U.S. Why it matters: U.S. tariffs aren’t just hurting Canada and Mexico, they’re also piling on new costs for American automakers — costs that the industry can scarcely afford as it scrambles to compete with cheaper (and, in some cases, higher quality) Chinese-made vehicles. Chinese auto brands are expected to grow their share of the global auto market to 33% by 2030, including 13% of the car market outside China What’s next: The White House is reportedly considering tariff relief for U.S. carmakers, but there’s no getting around the reality that if the Trump administration wants to force the industry to abandon Canada and Mexico, it’s going to have to make it pretty darn expensive to do business outside the U.S.—TS

    🇺🇸 🚚 25% tariffs on imported heavy-duty trucks — what could this mean for cross-border freight and the wider trucking industry? Earlier this month, President Trump confirmed that a new 25% tariff on imported medium and heavy-duty trucks will go into effect November 1, impacting major exporters such as Mexico, Canada, and Japan. In context, more than 40% of U.S. Class 8 trucks are imported from Canada and Mexico. Analysts predict prices for new heavy-duty trucks could jump by as much as $35,000, adding millions in annual operating costs for large fleets, potentially putting replacements out of reach for smaller carriers. The tariffs come against the backdrop of what many describe as the longest freight recession in modern memory. Rising operational costs (whether from equipment, parts, or cross-border delays) will likely push companies to search for offsets in other areas. Beyond direct equipment costs, the ripple effects could hit supply chains that have, for decades, benefited from integrated manufacturing between the U.S., Mexico, and Canada. Trade associations warn that prolonged tariffs could force production shifts, supply chain reengineering, and even job cuts. Whether these tariffs remain short-lived or persist into 2026, one thing is certain: trucking companies will be navigating yet another layer of uncertainty in an already complex operational landscape.
