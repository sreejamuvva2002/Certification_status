Why Your Next EV Battery Will Cost $700 More

And U.S. tariffs on Chinese graphite will be to blame.

Christopher Chico
Aug 27, 2025

Summary

  • U.S. tariffs on Chinese graphite are putting pressure on battery makers.

  • Battery Industry Pulse: weekly roundup.

Welcome back to another edition of my newsletter! - Week 34 2025

Graphite is a crucial component of lithium-ion batteries. It makes up about 30% to 40% of battery weight but only accounts for 8% to 15% of their cost. It serves as the main ingredient of the anode.

Graphite comes in two forms: natural (mined) and synthetic (manufactured from petroleum coke). These can also be blended.

The anode material used today is a high-purity and engineered graphite powder called active anode material (AAM).

In July 2025, the United States issued a preliminary anti-dumping duty of 93.5% on active anode material from China.

Importers must post cash deposits now while final decisions run through late 2025.

A separate anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese anode-grade graphite by the Commerce Department on May 20th resulted in a preliminary countervailing duty of 6.55% for most producers.

The goal is clear: cut reliance on China and push for more local supply.

The current market for graphite anode material

China completely dominates the global anode supply chain. The numbers are staggering.

The International Energy Agency estimates that China holds more than 97% of the world's production capacity to make active anode material. Most of the remainder sits in Korea and Japan.

Before the tariff, the U.S. imported most of its anode material from China. Commerce data show that in 2023, imports from China totaled about 84,000 tons, valued at roughly $347 million.

However, Rho Motion reported two significant trends emerging in 2025:

  • The Chinese Decline: In May 2025, U.S. imports from China fell to about 600 tons. The 2025 monthly average hovers around 5,500 to 6,000 tons, compared with more than 8,000 tons per month between 2022 and 2024.

  • The Indonesian Rise: Indonesia's AAM exports to the U.S. now average above 1,000 tons per month. These shipments are coming from BTR, a Chinese company that invested $478 million into the first phase of its Indonesian anode plant. The plant has an 80,000-ton capacity. BTR has confirmed that its Phase II anode material facility in Indonesia will begin operations in late 2025, adding 80,000 tons per year and doubling capacity to 160,000 tons per year. BTR is also planning an anode active material plant in Morocco with an annual capacity of 60,000 tons.

This reflects a broader trend of Chinese companies building factories overseas in Indonesia, Malaysia, Morocco, and Thailand to avoid potential tariffs on Chinese products.

However, governments are working to close these loopholes.

U.S. manufacturing of graphite and anode: who is active now

The petitioner in both the anti-dumping and anti-subsidy cases is the American Active Anode Material Producers, an ad hoc coalition of U.S. producers. It includes Anovion Technologies of Sanborn, New York, Syrah Technologies LLC of Vidalia, Louisiana, Novonix Anode Materials of Chattanooga, Tennessee, Epsilon Advanced Materials of Leland, North Carolina, and SKI US Inc. of Marietta, Georgia.

They allege that Chinese AAM products are subsidized and sold below “fair value”, harming U.S. industry. More importantly, they argue that battery manufacturers in the U.S. are not actively signing off-takes with U.S. AAM suppliers because Chinese prices are too low. This is impacting their industrialization progress, especially on getting capital.

According to the them, it’s a chicken-and-egg problem.

The current anode industry in the U.S. remains limited, with only a few active producers:

  • Syrah Resources, Vidalia, Louisiana: The first commercial-scale and integrated natural-graphite anode plant in the U.S. The facility started production in the first quarter of 2024 and has a capacity of 11,250 tons per year. It uses feedstock from Mozambique.

  • Novonix, Chattanooga, Tennessee: The Riverside plant has been installing and commissioning an initial 3,000 tpa synthetic graphite line, with an expansion to 20,000 tpa at Riverside over time and a larger Tennessee facility planned.

  • Anovion: The company operates a small facility of up to 5,000 tpa capacity today and is building toward a 40,000 tpa synthetic plant in Georgia later this decade with federal grant support. Pilot output exists and some commercial sales are reported, yet large automotive volumes depend on new capacity and full qualification.

Several other projects are in development, including Epsilon Advanced Materials and SKI US (Birla Carbon).

  • Epsilon Advanced Materials plans to build a $650-million graphite anode manufacturing facility in Brunswick County, North Carolina.

  • SKI US (Birla Carbon) will invest $1 billion to build a synthetic graphite production facility in South Carolina. The project also received a $150-million grant from the U.S. Department of Energy for its initial phase.

But these projects are not yet operational.

Beyond the U.S., Canada has the only active natural graphite mine in North America. It is owned by Northern Graphite, which produces around 10,000 to 15,000 tons per year of graphite concentrate.

Nouveau Monde Graphite is advancing an integrated mine-to-anode project near Bécancour with a design case of roughly 42,000 tons per year of coated spherical graphite.

Yet, the gap will remain wide due to the lack of current capacity.

How the tariffs change the market

The preliminary anti-dumping rate of 93.5% applies on top of a preliminary subsidy duty and other existing tariffs on China. This brings the total tariff on most Chinese AAM imports to about 160%. This figure fundamentally alters market dynamics. The new duties also cover the AAM contained in imported battery cells.

BMI estimates that tariffed Chinese AAM that previously landed near $3,700 per ton would now land near $9,300 per ton. U.S. material is estimated at around $5,400 per ton today. That completely flips the cost curve.

The impact on battery costs is immediate and substantial. Tariffed Chinese AAM would add roughly $5 to $7 per kWh at the cell level. A battery with a capacity of 100 kWh would therefore cost around $500 to $700 more to purchase.

Battery makers will be the first to feel these additional costs, but the impact won't stop there.

Besides scale challenges, U.S. AAM suppliers are not yet ready on technical specifications. In proceedings around the trade case, Tesla spoke out against the graphite tariffs, arguing that it was dependent on Chinese imports because the U.S. industry was not developed enough to meet the quality standards and quantities required by the carmaker.

High tariffs without a plan are risky

I have mixed feelings about these very high tariffs on Chinese AAM imports. I have two specific reasons:

  • First, there is not enough capacity in the U.S. to meet current demand, and it will take time to build the supply chain. Battery manufacturers will face immediate pressure and have to deal with constant uncertainty as U.S. tariff policies keep changing.

    Imports of Chinese AAM have already decreased, but Indonesia is taking the lead now, with the main player still being a Chinese company (BTR). Key players keep the priority over U.S suppliers in the short term.

  • Second, the increased prices will affect battery makers’ and OEMs’ profitability or be passed to the final customer. You might say it is just a few hundred dollars in additional cost. Yes, but if you add tariffs on other imported products from China, Korea, Japan, and Indonesia, the total can reach thousands.

This is not a positive trend for the EV market. The U.S. EV market needs more affordable vehicles to increase adoption, not additional cost pressures.

If I were in their position, I would still push for a domestic supply chain. I would also use tariffs, but not at this extreme level.

Instead, I would match U.S. prices in the first year, then gradually increase tariffs each year to align with the ramp-up timeline of local producers.

That approach would allow battery manufacturers to absorb smaller price increases in the short term while being better prepared to work closely with local AAM suppliers in the following years.

People should remember that building an entire supply chain takes decades. Europe and the U.S. sometimes forget this fundamental reality. Korea, Japan, and China have been in the game since the 1990s.
