      Tariff Resource Center for Contractors

      Note: The tariff situation is constantly evolving. This page was last updated on 8/25/2026. 

      Overview

      Tariffs are taxes imposed by governments on imported goods to protect domestic industries or generate revenue. They can be percentage-based or flat fees per unit. These taxes are paid at the point of entry into the United States by the importer of the products and affect the pricing of goods. Steel, aluminum, lumber, and electrical components are just some of the examples of the wide variety of construction materials that can be affected by import taxes and experience price fluctuations.

      To help contractors navigate challenges related to possible tariffs, AGC is creating resources, educating the construction community, and advocating for contractors on key considerations on the potential impacts as it relates to construction.

      AGC of America is working to provide contractors with the most up-to-date information on tariffs. Make sure to continue to check this page for the latest resources created to help contractors navigate tariffs.

      Tariffs in Effect

      *NEW* Drone Tariffs - 15% - 100% tariff rate on drones and their components, based on specific components, weight, and country of origin. Click here to find out more.

      *NEW* Forced Labor Section 301 Tariffs - 10% - 12.5% tariffs on the top 60 U.S. trade partners, covering 99.4% of U.S. imports. 

      *NEW* Canada Tariffs - 50% tariffs on specific goods imported from Canada, including cement, paint, plywood, fiber cable and more.

      Steel, Aluminum, and Copper - 50% tariffs on items made entirely or mostly from the listed metals

      • 25% tariffs on derivatives made substantially of steel, aluminum, and copper
      • 15% tariff on industrial and electrical grid equipment that contains steel, aluminum, and copper
      • 10% tariff on products made abroad, but entirely with American steel, aluminum, and copper
      • 15% tariff on agricultural and industrial equipment (such as bulldozers and forklifts)
      • 15% tariff on HVAC systems and components

      Softwood Lumber and Timber - 10% tariff.

      • Various derivatives of lumber and timber are set at a 25% tariff.

      Automobiles and Parts - 25% tariff on all imported automobiles and specific automobile parts. 

      Tariffs No Longer in Effect Due To Supreme Court Decision

      Reciprocal Tariffs -  Ranging from 15 - 40%. Reciprocal tariffs on most countries around the world.

      • If a country is not subject to reciprocal tariffs, they are still subject to the 10% universal tariff (except Canada and Mexico).

      China - 30%

      • President Donald Trump imposed tariffs on imports from China that added an additional 10% tax on all goods coming into the United States. On March 4, 2025, he increased those tariffs by an additional 10%. These tariffs combine with the universal tariffs for a total of 30% import tax on all Chinese goods, in addition to any other tariffs that might be in place for specific products. China and the US are currently negotiating terms for a trade deal, during which the initially set high tariff rate of 125% has been paused.

      Mexico - 25% tariff on goods that are not USMCA compliant.

      • These tariffs affect about 50% of all imports from Mexico. The baseline and reciprocal tariffs mentioned above do not currently affect Mexico.

      Canada - 35% tariff on all non-USMCA compliant products. Energy and critical minerals are at a lower 10% rate. 

      • These tariffs, which currently affect about 40% of all imports from Canada, are currently in effect. The baseline and reciprocal tariffs mentioned above do not currently affect Canada.

      Additional Country Specific Tariffs - President Trump has imposed tariffs on multiple countries in addition to already existing tariffs.

      • Brazil – 40%
      • India – 25%

      Purchasers of Venezuelan Petroleum - President Trump signed an Executive Order that allows the Secretary of State to investigate countries which purchase petroleum from Venezuela and impose a 25% tax on them. The purchase could be directly or through a third party. Currently, China and the US are the two major purchasers of Venezuelan oil. This executive order is effective starting April 2, 2025. However, the executive order is worded in such a way that these tariffs are discretionary based on the Secretary of State's judgement and decision. No current tariffs based on this executive order.

      Trade Deals

      The following countries have specific trade deals that have various exemptions or rates separate from the ones above.

      • Japan – 15%
      • EU – 15%
      • UK – 10%
      • Philippines – 19%
      • Indonesia – 19%
      • Vietnam – 20%; 40% on transshipment
      • South Korea - 15%

      Tariff Stacking

        Initially, all the tariffs were worded in such a manner that the rates would stack on top of each other. For example, Steel (at a tariff rate of 25%) from Canada (at a tariff rate of 25%) would combine to be subject to a 50% total tariff rate. The Trump Administration has clarified that the following tariffs do not stack with each other, but still do stack with the rest of the tariff actions.

        • Mexico
        • Canada
        • Steel
        • Aluminum
        • Autos and Auto Parts

        Potential Future Tariffs

        The Trump Administration has numerous ongoing investigations into potential tariffs on the products and materials below. These investigations can take nearly a year from beginning to imposition of tariffs and it is not guaranteed that these tariffs will take effect. 

        • All goods imported into the United States from Russia at a rate of up to 500% ad valorem.
        • Critical Minerals - Includes downstream products that cover "all goods that incorporate processed critical minerals as inputs".
        • Robotics and Industrial Machinery 
        • PPE and Medical Equipment
        • Semiconductors - Semiconductors and their downstream products. Downstream products would involve any products that contain semiconductors. There is currently no specific timeline of when the investigation's report will be released and whether tariffs will actually be imposed.
        • Heavy Trucks 
        • Polysilicon
        • Unmanned Aircraft Systems 
        • Commercial Aircraft

        Impact of Tariffs

        Tariffs have long been a critical tool in U.S. trade policy, affecting the construction industry in both direct and indirect ways. These measures are aimed at addressing trade imbalances and incentivizing domestic production, but they also carry risks of escalating costs, retaliatory trade measures, and further complicating global supply chains. For construction contractors, such policies could lead to stockpiling of materials, reconsideration of contracts, and pricing and scheduling uncertainty.

        To have a better sense of what might be affected in a specific market with potential tariffs, contractors need to be aware of what products are imported and how much. A key consideration for contractors is that domestic equivalents to imported materials can experience a shift in additional demand due to tariffs and thus cause the price of domestic materials to fluctuate as well.

        • Find out how much your state imports from any country (U.S. International Trade Administration)
        • Learn more about trade between the United States and Canada (Government of Canada)
        • Learn more about trade between the United States and Mexico (Embassy of Mexico)
        • Learn more about each mineral, how much the US produces and imports (US Geological Survey)

        Tools and Resources

        Contracts:  ConsensusDocs Price Escalation Resources 

        Tariffs are likely to further contribute to volatility in the construction materials market. ConsensusDocs, has compiled resources to address price escalation. The best approach is to include price escalation provision, such as the ConsensusDocs 200.1 Material Price Escalation Amendment in your construction agreement at contract signing.

        Federal and Federal-Aid Contracts: AGC of America's Tariff Memo

        AGC developed a Tariff Memo outlining key considerations and potential impacts of the administration’s trade policies on the construction industry. It covers general circumstances that construction contractors may face in connection with an increase in tariffs and how contractors can work through them.

        Import Trade Data: DataWeb: U.S. Trade & Tariff Data

        The USITC DataWeb provides public access to the official U.S. import and export statistics of the U.S. Department of Commerce in a user-friendly web interface. Using the DataWeb querying tool, users can build custom queries and access these data in a spreadsheet or a web-based format to search for specific materials and how they might impact your business.
