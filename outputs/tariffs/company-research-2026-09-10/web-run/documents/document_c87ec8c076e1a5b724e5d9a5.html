Georgia and Tariffs Georgia is a critical supply chain hub. It is one of the most trade-dependent states in the U.S., serving as a major logistics, import, and export hub. Its ports, manufacturing base, and distribution infrastructure mean that both U.S. tariffs on imports and foreign tariffs on U.S. exports hits Georgia especially hard—raising costs for businesses and consumers while threatening export-driven industries. Some Trade Facts: - GDP Impact: Trade accounts for ~21% of Georgia’s $888B economy, significantly larger than the US average. - Savannah Port: 4th busiest U.S. container port; $91B in import value. - Brunswick Port: #1 U.S. auto import port; ~900K vehicles imported annually. - Atlanta Logistics Hub: Global air cargo gateway + rail + interstate access. - $136.6B in Imports (2023): Electronics, vehicles, machinery. - $49.7B in Exports (2023): Aircraft, autos, poultry, chemicals. - Top Trade Partners: China, Mexico, South Korea, Canada, Germany. - Trade-Related Jobs: 800,000+ tied to ports, logistics, and manufacturing.

    Last week the European Union announced that its tariff-free steel import quota would drop from 33mtpa to just 18.3mtpa, a fall of 47%, whilst steel imports in excess of that quota would now be tariffed at 50% (up from 25%), with effect from June 2026.   The tariffs are largely a response to the US’s own 50% tariffs on EU steel implemented 1st June (which were increased from the initial 25% tariffs imposed in March). The US is the largest importer of EU steel, accounting for 20% of the EU’s steel exports (4.2mt of 19.5mt exported in 2024, and 2.3mt of 10.5mt exported between January and July this year).   The biggest impact, however, may well be felt by the UK as the country exports around 80% of its steel (1.9mtpa) to the EU, albeit the EU has announced it is open to negotiations with the UK once the WTO is notified of the new levy.   The EU’s Commissioner for Trade and Economic Security recently announced that 30,000 jobs have been lost from the EU’s steel industry since 2018 – about 10 percent of the steel workforce. More starkly, and un-mentioned by the Commissioner, is that EU steel output has declined from 209mt at the 2007 peak to 168mt in 2018 and 130mt last year. Production over January–August this year has fallen a further -3.7 percent y-o-y from 87.7mt to 84.5mt.   Looking forward, another key headwind is the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is transitioning from reporting to implementation in January 2026, meaning importers will be required to supply CBAM certificates outlining carbon emissions of imports.   Overall, the implementation should favour cleaner steel exporters to the EU (e.g. Turkey and Japan), as well as domestic producers, thereby indirectly favouring exporters of iron ore and scrap into the EU (e.g. Canada, Brazil, the US and West Africa).
