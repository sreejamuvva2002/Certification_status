How auto tariffs affect car prices: What you need to know

Learn how recent tariff changes may affect your search - and what you can do about it. Used cars are not directly affected by tariffs.

LAST UPDATED: December 18, 2025

Understanding auto tariffs

What are tariffs?
  • Taxes imposed on imported goods
  • Additional fees on vehicles or parts manufactured outside the country
  • Directly increase the final cost to consumers
Impact on car prices
  • Manufacturers typically pass costs to consumers
  • Price increases range from hundreds to thousands of dollars
  • Impact varies based on vehicle origin and components
Implementation timeline
  • Most recent changes effective within 30 days of announcement
  • Some manufacturers adjust pricing in anticipation
  • Others might absorb costs temporarily

Are used cars affected by tariffs?

If you're buying a used car that's already in the U.S. and was originally sold here (even if it’s a foreign brand), tariffs do not apply to your purchase. The cost may reflect earlier tariff impacts on parts or the original new car, but you won’t be charged a tariff at the used car lot.

FAQs

As of April 3, 2025, the U.S. imposes a 25% additional tariff on imported passenger vehicles and light trucks, in addition to the existing 2.5% base tariff. This results in a total tariff of 27.5% on passenger cars and 50% on light trucks from countries without a U.S. free trade agreement. These tariffs increase the cost of imported vehicles, leading to higher prices for consumers. Buyers may see reduced availability of certain foreign-made vehicles or price hikes as manufacturers and dealers pass on the increased import costs.
Vehicle prices have risen an estimated 10–15% as a result of the new tariffs. According to the Anderson Economic Group, tariffs have added at least $2,000 to the cost of an average vehicle. Other forecasts estimate price increases of $5,000 to $10,000 for some foreign brands, and around $3,000 for domestically produced vehicles affected by global supply chains. The Budget Lab at Yale further estimates the average overall increase at approximately $6,400 per vehicle. These figures underscore the financial impact tariffs can have on both imported and U.S.-manufactured cars. In April 2025, new vehicle pricing experienced an increase of 2.5% month-over-month, more than double the typical monthly rise of 1.1% observed over the past several years. At the same time, sales incentives dropped to their lowest level as a percentage of transaction price since the summer of 2024, signaling a tightening market and reduced dealer discounting.
Used vehicles are not directly subject to tariffs since the duties apply to new vehicle imports. However, the ripple effects of higher new car prices can significantly impact the used car market. As tariffs drive up the cost of new vehicles, demand for used cars often rises, putting upward pressure on prices in the pre-owned market. Over time, this can lead to notable price increases for used vehicles, especially for in-demand makes and models.
Generally, dealerships pass tariff-related price increases to consumers. However, during competitive periods or inventory clearance events, dealers might temporarily absorb some costs to meet sales targets. The overall impact varies depending on the dealership's strategy and market conditions.

Auto tariffs can have a direct impact on dealership inventory and vehicle availability. Higher tariffs increase the cost of importing certain models, leading to fewer choices for consumers and longer wait times for custom orders. Additionally, tariffs on auto parts can disrupt supply chains, affecting the availability of both new and used vehicles.
When auto tariffs are active, vehicle prices—especially for imported models—can fluctuate. Shoppers should be prepared for higher prices on certain brands and trims, particularly those manufactured in countries without U.S. trade agreements. To minimize cost impact, consider these tips: • Act quickly: Many dealers are promoting “tariff-free” vehicles, which are units imported before the tariffs were enacted. Once this inventory is sold, newly imported vehicles will reflect the higher tariff-adjusted prices. • Consider certified pre-owned (CPO) vehicles: CPO models may provide better value and are unaffected by new import duties. • Look at domestic brands or tariff-exempt sources: Vehicles built in the U.S. or imported from countries with trade agreements may offer more price stability. • Stay informed: Keeping up with trade policy developments, dealer incentives, and inventory updates can help you make a timely, well-informed purchase. In short, timing and awareness are key. The sooner you buy—particularly from existing stock—the better your chances of avoiding post-tariff price increases.
As of May 8, 2025, the United States imposed a 25% tariff on imported vehicles and auto parts from Canada and Mexico. However, vehicles and parts that comply with the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) are exempt from these tariffs. This exemption has implications for North American trade: • Mexico, as the largest exporter of auto parts to the U.S., continues to benefit under the USMCA, helping to preserve its competitive pricing and maintain smooth integration with U.S. manufacturers. • The exemption also supports the North American automotive supply chain, reducing potential disruptions and allowing vehicle production and repairs in the U.S. to remain more cost-effective. For consumers and businesses, this means Canadian and Mexican vehicles and parts that meet USMCA rules of origin can avoid tariff-related price increases, helping stabilize costs in a volatile market.

Tariff A government-imposed tax on goods imported from other countries. In the context of autos, tariffs raise the cost of foreign vehicles and parts entering the U.S., often leading to higher prices for consumers. Base Tariff The pre-existing standard tariff on imported vehicles—typically 2.5% in the U.S. for passenger cars. It applies unless superseded or increased by additional trade actions. Additional Tariff An extra duty imposed on top of the base tariff, often as part of broader trade policies. For example, a 25% additional tariff was added to many vehicle imports in April 2025, raising total duties to 27.5% or more. Certified Pre-Owned (CPO) A used vehicle that has been inspected, refurbished, and certified by a manufacturer or dealer, often including an extended warranty. CPO vehicles offer value and peace of mind and are not affected by new tariffs. USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement) A trade agreement that replaced NAFTA in 2020, designed to maintain free trade among the U.S., Mexico, and Canada. Vehicles and parts that meet USMCA’s rules of origin can be imported tariff-free between member nations. Rules of Origin Requirements under trade agreements that specify the minimum percentage of parts or production that must originate from within the participating countries (e.g., USMCA members) for a product to qualify for tariff exemptions. Quota A limit on the quantity of goods that can be imported under favorable tariff terms. For example, under the U.S.-UK agreement, only 100,000 British vehicles per year qualify for the reduced 10% tariff.

Volkswagen has paused some shipments to the U.S. in response to the tariffs and has guaranteed that prices will remain stable through the month of June to assuage consumer concerns. They have signaled that should the tariffs extend beyond that, they will not be able to absorb all of the added cost.

Pricing impact

Pricing will remain unchanged on all Volkswagen models until the end of June.

Production impact

The company has paused rail shipments of Mexican-built cars into the U.S., but it’s continuing water shipments from Mexico and Europe. However, any car delivered to an American port after the tariffs take effect will be held.

Volvo

Manufacturer response

Volvo is removing the S90 sedan from its U.S. lineup due to the new 125 percent tariff on China-made vehicles. Given the S90's low sales volume and production location in China, Volvo has decided to discontinue the model. The company plans to focus more heavily on its top-selling SUVs, and is also considering expanding production at its South Carolina plant to offset future tariff impacts.

Pricing impact

No direct price increases have been announced yet, but the removal of the S90 and other potential changes may signal that Volvo is adjusting its lineup instead of passing tariff costs directly to customers.

Production impact

The S90 will be discontinued for the U.S. market after the 2025 model year, and Volvo is evaluating plans to scale up production at its South Carolina facility to strengthen its U.S. manufacturing footprint.

Volvo's EV, the EX30, has begun production at its Belgian factory, to avoid the steeper Chinese tariffs, where the EX30 was previously being manufactured.

Volvo is moving production of the XC60 SUV from Sweden to its plant in Ridgeville, South Carolina.
