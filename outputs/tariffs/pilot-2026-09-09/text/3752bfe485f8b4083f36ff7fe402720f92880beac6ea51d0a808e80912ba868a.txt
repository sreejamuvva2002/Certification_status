Abstract
Despite the U.S. climate ambitions requiring a 24% annual growth in EV sales, its EV market has experienced slow growth. Affordable EV imports could help break this stagnation, but the U.S. imposed high tariffs, to protect domestic auto industry. We argue that localizing foreign production capacity in the U.S. could reignite the electrification momentum without sacrificing other priorities. This strategy is also crucial for many other countries facing similar dilemma.
Introduction
Stagnating U.S. EV Market
The U.S. climate transition has hit a roadblock. Its climate ambition of achieving 50% Electric Vehicle (EV) market penetration by 2030 mandates a 24% annual growth in EV sales. However, the U.S. EV market has experienced slow growth for over 20 consecutive months (Fig. 1)1. Similar trend is observed in many other countries. France, Germany, and Italy saw stagnation or even decline in EV adoption since Q3 2023, far below the 27% annual growth needed to meet net-zero emission targets2. In contrast, EV adoption is accelerating on the other side of the globe. China’s EV penetration rate has exceeded 50% since July 2024. Australia, with a similar EV penetration level to the U.S., experiences 44% growth in EV sales from Q3 2023 to Q2 2025.
The slowdown in the U.S. stems from multiple factors, including range anxiety3, battery safety concerns4, infrastructure deficiencies5, high interest rate6, saturation of company car fleet7, etc. While many of these issues exist globally, China’s sustained growth demonstrates that offering highly affordable EVs could potentially help markets overcome these challenges and sustain organic market growth.
Chinese automakers currently offer some of the world’s most affordable EVs. China’s BYD Seal, a top-selling mid-size EV in Australia, is priced 22% below its American counterpart – Tesla Model 3. BYD’s advanced Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicle (PHEV) model, the Qin L, offers 81-mpg fuel economy and a 2100-km range (80-km all-electric range)8, all for $13,800 – half the price of the U.S. PHEV sales leader, Toyota Prius. These advantages help explain the rapid EV adoption in China and countries welcoming Chinese EVs, such as Australia.
The U.S. is tightening market access for foreign EVs. Taking Chinese EVs as an example, in May 2024, the Biden administration imposed a 100% additional tariff on Chinese EV imports. Subsequently, the Trump administration raised this tariff substantially to 145%-245%, before negotiations reduced it to 135%. Similar tariff increases have appeared in U.S. trade with many other countries. It is, therefore, important to analyze the impact of the tariffs from a cost perspective.
EV Tariff Impact Assessment
In this study, we first analyze the potential effect of the increased tariffs from the consumer perspective, then argue that this move risks forfeiting a critical opportunity for the U.S. to enhance EV affordability, stimulate market growth, and hit climate targets. Finally, we discuss potential strategies to improve outcomes. Our assessment is based on a Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) framework2,9, which measures the cost of purchasing and using a vehicle.
Given the uncertainty surrounding future tariff rates following potential negotiations and the already substantial effect of the 100% additional tariff, we use the scenario of Chinese EVs under the 100% additional tariff to illustrate the impacts. Considering the limited profit margin of Chinese automakers, full tariff pass-through is assumed in the baseline case, with varying pass-through rates tested in sensitivity analysis. Using mid-size cars – the largest segment in the U.S. market – as a representative example, we compare the TCO of Battery Electric Vehicles (BEVs) with that of Hybrid Electric Vehicles (HEVs), the major competitors to BEVs.
The results indicate that BEVs currently do not offer a significant TCO advantage against HEVs in the U.S. For most states, the TCO of HEVs and BEVs are close (Fig. 2a), and BEVs even cost $387-2582 higher than HEVs in more than 10 states. This partly explains the limited momentum to overcome the current EV market stagnation. A comparison across states without Zero-Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandates further reveals that EV penetration rates are generally higher in states where BEVs provide clear TCO savings. For instance, in Arizona, Utah, and Illinois – where BEVs save $4679, $3178, and $2287 over HEVs in the lifetime, respectively – the EV penetration rates reached 9.4%, 8.7%, and 7.8% in 2024 (Fig. 2b).
If China-produced EVs were introduced into the U.S. market without additional tariffs, they could significantly enhance EV affordability in all 50 states (Fig. 2c). China-produced BEVs could save $10,000-20,000 over their lifecycle compared with HEVs, substantially more than U.S.-made BEVs ($12,500-15,000 more saved). This price advantage could potentially trigger a surge in EV adoption in the U.S., akin to the situation seen in Australia. Even with higher electricity prices and potential battery replacements, Chinese EVs would still save $6000-16,000 compared with HEVs. Such affordability-improvement benefits can be mirrored across all other segments (Fig. 2d), particularly in small and compact vehicle segments. These segments accounted for over 37.8% of the U.S. auto market, yet exhibited EV penetration rates of only 4.5%, far below the fleet average of 11.6%. Limited model availability and high prices have been key barriers. More affordable options could unlock substantial market growth in these segments.
However, China-produced EVs no longer offer affordability benefits under high U.S. tariffs. (Fig. 2e). Using representative mid-size car models as an example (Fig. 2f), the TCO of BYD Seal would have been 19% and 20% lower than that of Tesla Model 3 and Toyota Camry Hybrid. With the tariff, these numbers reverse, making the BYD Seal 19% and 17% more costly. Assuming lower tariff pass-through rates of 50% and 75%, the TCO of mid-size Chinese EVs would be only 2% lower and 7.8% higher than that of HEVs (Fig. 3a). Under alternative assumptions for energy prices, maintenance costs, and battery degradation, additional tariffs would raise the TCO of Chinese EVs to 16%-21% higher than that of HEVs (Fig. 3b).
This situation is not unique. EVs from Japan, Korea, and the EU also face tariffs exceeding 25%, further reducing the affordability of EV products in the U.S. This means that the U.S. has to rely on the affordability of domestically produced EVs to incentivize consumer purchases, whereas EV sales suggest this strategy has not been successful. Moreover, the up-to-$7500 tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), which primarily benefit U.S.-made EVs, are set to expire in September 2025. This could further erode the EV affordability, creating additional barriers to EV market growth in the U.S.
A Win-Win Solution
While consumers experience welfare losses from tariffs, several stakeholders stand to benefit. The automotive industry provides 9.7 million jobs in the U.S10., accounting for 2.8% of the total employment. In swing states like Michigan, 20% of the workforce is employed in the auto sector11. Imposing EV tariffs could safeguard domestic manufacturing and employment, enable U.S. automakers retain substantial market shares, strengthen political support in key states, and increase government revenues.
However, these gains come at the expense of the low-carbon transition and long-term industrial competitiveness. Elevated tariffs hinder access to affordable EV imports, slowing progress toward decarbonization. Reduced market competition may also weaken incentives for innovation and efficiency improvements among U.S. automakers. The U.S. exported 2.5 million cars in 202312. More than half of the production from U.S. automakers were sold in overseas markets, where they face direct competition without tariff protection. Additionally, U.S.-based suppliers, such as Goodyear, Lear, and Qualcomm, produce parts for automakers in China, Japan, and the EU. Tariff escalation risks retaliation and complicates their international operations.
Localizing foreign production capacities, e.g., Chinese EV supply chains, in the U.S. offers a more balanced strategy. First, it helps preserve the affordability of Chinese EVs even with U.S.-based production, thereby supporting vehicle electrification. BYD’s electric bus plant in Lancaster, California, the world’s largest electric bus manufacturing facility, exemplifies this. Electric buses produced there, with 70% U.S. content by value13, cost 10%-30% less than U.S.-made alternatives14. Second, localized production generates social and economic benefits for local communities. BYD’s Lancaster plant has contributed to a 25% increase in the city’s annual revenue and a 35% growth in local employment, demonstrating the synergy of collaboration between Chinese firms and U.S. supply chains. Third, localizing Chinese EV supply chain can foster technological spillovers, talent development, and market competition, incentivizing U.S. automakers to innovate, improve EV affordability, and strengthen global competitiveness.
The global EV trade is facing rising barriers. The EU imposed 17%-38% tariffs on Chinese EV imports. Mexico has removed EV import tax exemptions for countries without free trade agreements. Canada and Turkey also imposed protectionist policies. In this context, we argue that, to meet EV penetration targets, partnering with Chinese companies to build local supply chains can be a feasible strategy for these countries. The affordability advantage of Chinese EVs can be largely maintained with localized manufacturing. For example, the cost of BYD Seal produced in Hungary would be 25% lower than its Tesla counterpart15. Moreover, this can be achieved without sacrificing those countries core interests.
The challenges facing China-produced EVs are not isolated incidents. Amid complex political, economic, and technology competition contexts, global manufacturing and supply chain are increasingly fragmented. Particularly, trade barriers to critical and strategic technologies such as batteries, photovoltaics, and semiconductors are all on the rise16. Many of these technologies are essential to low-carbon transition. We argue that international cooperation between foreign advanced production capacity and local supply chains should be strengthened to achieve the co-benefits in climate change mitigation, domestic social welfare, and industrial competitiveness.
Methods
TCO Calculation Model
The TCO approach is widely used and well established in techno-economic analyses17,18. It accounts for all costs incurred over the lifecycle of a technology and can be applied across a broad range of technologies. In this study, we developed a TCO model to assess the impact of U.S. tariffs on consumers. The TCO of vehicles comprises of both purchasing cost and using cost, including depreciation cost, license and registration cost, insurance, maintenance and repair cost, and fuel cost (Eq. 1). The tariffs add the depreciation cost and is presented separately in this study. In the U.S., the tariffs include the basic tariff, the Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF), the Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF), and the additional tariff, which is assumed to be 100% due to its already significant effect.

The depreciation cost is calculated by multiplying the price and the depreciation rate that declines over time (Eq. 2). The license and registration cost varies by state and is derived from regulatory data across all 50 U.S. states. The insurance is the sum of a fixed base rate and a variable portion that also differs by state (Eq. 3). The maintenance and repair cost is obtained based on the average per-km rate and the total mileage traveled (Eq. 4). The fuel cost is calculated as the product of energy consumption and fuel price (Eq. 5). For PHEVs, energy consumption includes both electricity and gasoline, which are calculated based on the Utility Factor (UF)9. Key Assumptions and Considerations
In this study, we chose representative models for TCO calculation. All vehicle models are categorized into 10 segments based on the wheelbase, i.e., cars: mini, small, compact, mid-size, and large; SUV: mini, small, compact, mid-size, and large19. The representative models are the top sellers in each segment. Detailed specifications of these models, including the price, energy consumption rate, and the wheelbase, are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The TCO results are listed in Supplementary Table S4–S13. Abbreviations used in this paper, along with the full names, are listed in Supplementary S15 and S16.
The tariffs’ impacts are complex, involving multiple stakeholders and heterogeneous effects20. For consumers, tariffs lead to welfare losses, whereas certain industries may benefit from protectionist measures. Given the difficulty of directly comparing these diverse costs and benefits, we adopt TCO as the primary indicator to assess the outcomes of tariff policies. It is also related to broader welfare dimensions, such as low-carbon transition. A more comprehensive assessment framework would, however, require further in-depth analysis.
Data availability
The EV sales and market penetration rate data are derived from Marklines, an online database. The prices and energy consumption rates of the representative vehicle models are obtained on the official website of the automakers. The depreciation rate, insurance, license and registration cost, and the maintenance and repair cost are derived from the Argonne National Laboratory’s AFLEET Tool model. The fuel prices in 50 states and other data are derived from online database.
