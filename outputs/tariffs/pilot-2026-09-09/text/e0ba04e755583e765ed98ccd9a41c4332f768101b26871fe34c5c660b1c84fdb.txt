1. What Happened: The Tariff Timeline Every OEM Needs to Know
The tariff landscape affecting battery pack manufacturing has shifted dramatically since 2024. For OEM engineers and supply chain managers who were not tracking every policy update, the cumulative effect has been jarring. Here is the timeline that matters.
2024: The Foundation Is Set
The Biden administration established the framework that the Trump administration would later expand. Section 301 tariffs on Chinese battery products – including cells, packs, and key materials – were formalized and expanded. The stage was set for a much more aggressive trade posture.
January 2025: The Cascade Begins
Multiple tariff measures took effect simultaneously. A 93.5 percent tariff on Chinese graphite – the primary anode material in virtually all lithium-ion batteries – took effect, representing one of the most significant raw material tariffs in US manufacturing history. A 30 percent baseline tariff on all Chinese goods compounded the impact on battery cells and pack components sourced from China. Steel and copper tariffs of 50 percent added further cost pressure on pack housings, connectors, busbars, and wiring harnesses.
Mid-2025: The Policy Accelerates
Section 232 investigations into battery-related imports were announced, signaling additional potential tariff actions beyond existing measures. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act eliminated federal EV purchase incentives – the $7,500 new EV tax credit, the $4,000 used EV credit, and the commercial fleet EV credit – reducing downstream demand pressure but doing nothing to reduce upstream input costs for battery manufacturers.
2026: FEOC Compliance Kicks In
Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) regulations began applying to battery supply chains, creating compliance obligations around Chinese-sourced battery materials that go beyond cost into legal risk territory. We cover FEOC in detail in Section 8.
Key Context: The Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) in February 2026, but the administration continued pursuing battery-related trade barriers through other legal authorities including Section 232 investigations, while maintaining existing measures including a 10 percent import surcharge under Section 122. The regulatory environment remains fluid.
 
2. The Real Cost of Battery Pack Tariffs on OEM Sourcing
Abstract tariff percentages matter less to OEM program managers than the actual dollar impact on battery pack pricing. Here is what the data shows.
The Headline Numbers
Battery storage costs in the US have risen between 56 and 69 percent since January 2025, according to Cleanview CEO Michael Thomas, cited in research from the Pew Research Center and industry publications. This is not a rounding error – for OEMs running programs with multi-year bill-of-materials assumptions, a 60 percent input cost increase can eliminate program profitability entirely.
BloombergNEF data tells a more nuanced story at the cell level: average global battery pack prices fell approximately 3 percent to $105 per kilowatt-hour in 2025, driven by Chinese manufacturing overcapacity that continued to push prices down globally. However, that global average masked dramatically different experiences by region. North American pack prices fell only 4 percent, while Chinese manufacturers absorbed cost pressure through aggressive pricing strategies aimed at maintaining market share in export markets. For US OEMs sourcing domestically compliant supply chains, the experience was sharply negative.

3. How the US Battery Supply Chain Is Shifting
The tariff environment is accelerating structural changes in battery supply chains that were already underway. Understanding these shifts is essential for OEMs making long-term sourcing decisions in 2026 and beyond.
Reshoring and Nearshoring Momentum
Domestic battery manufacturing in the US is growing, driven by a combination of tariff pressure, defense procurement requirements, and residual IRA investment momentum that was locked in before the subsidies were eliminated. Panasonic, Samsung SDI, LG Energy Solution, and SK On all have or are building US cell manufacturing capacity. However, the US battery manufacturing base remains heavily dependent on imported raw materials and components – particularly graphite, lithium carbonate, and cathode active materials – which means “made in America” batteries often still carry significant tariff exposure through their supply chains.
Korean and Japanese Cells as the Middle Path
For OEMs who cannot access domestic US cells at competitive prices, Korean and Japanese cell manufacturers have become the preferred alternative to Chinese cells. These suppliers carry lower tariff burdens, strong quality reputations, and established supply chains into the US market. Totex maintains relationships with Tier 1 cell manufacturers from both regions and can source cells from multiple origins depending on program requirements. The China Manufacturing Question Has Not Gone Away
Despite tariff pressure, China’s battery manufacturing ecosystem remains the world’s most advanced and cost-competitive for most cell chemistries and pack configurations. Chinese manufacturers have responded to Western tariff pressure by aggressively expanding into Southeast Asian markets, building manufacturing presence in countries like Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Hungary to create tariff-advantaged supply routes into the US and European markets. This “China+1” strategy is now a standard consideration in battery supply chain design.

Supply Chain Transparency Is Now a Compliance Requirement
The days of treating battery supply chains as a black box – “we buy cells from a distributor, we don’t know where they came from” – are over. FEOC regulations and DoD procurement requirements now demand traceability to the mine level for certain programs. This is a significant operational change for many OEMs and their battery suppliers. 4. The Chemistry Shift Tariffs Are Accelerating
One of the less obvious consequences of the tariff environment is the acceleration of a chemistry shift that was already underway for performance and cost reasons. The tariff structure is creating new economic incentives that are changing which battery chemistries make sense for different OEM applications.
LFP: The Big Winner in the Tariff Era
Lithium Iron Phosphate (LiFePO4, or LFP) has become the dominant chemistry in global battery markets – accounting for over 81 percent of market share by volume in 2025 according to CIBF 2026 data – and the tariff environment is reinforcing this dominance. LFP’s advantages in a tariff-constrained environment include:
- No cobalt or nickel in the cathode, eliminating exposure to DRC export quotas and the premium chemistry tariff exposure that affects NMC
- Lower raw material cost per kWh even before tariff considerations
- Thermal runaway threshold above 500 degrees C – superior inherent safety reduces the regulatory burden in medical and defense applications
- Cycle life of 3,000 to 10,000 charges – dramatically longer than NMC, reducing total cost of ownership for industrial and medical applications where battery replacement is expensive or operationally disruptive
OEM Design Consideration: LFP’s very flat discharge curve makes state-of-charge estimation challenging for battery management systems. This is a solvable engineering problem but requires a more sophisticated BMS than typical NMC packs. Totex’s in-house firmware team has developed robust SOC estimation algorithms for LFP applications across medical, industrial, and aerospace platforms. Do not assume a BMS designed for NMC will work correctly with LFP cells.
 
Sodium-Ion: The Next Chemistry to Watch
Sodium-ion batteries have no lithium, cobalt, or nickel – making them entirely insulated from the tariff pressures affecting lithium-based chemistries. Industry analysis projects sodium-ion could capture 30 to 40 percent of the stationary storage market within five years. For OEM applications, sodium-ion is currently best suited for lower-energy applications in controlled temperature environments – industrial equipment, stationary backup power, urban delivery vehicles – rather than high-density portable applications. The global sodium-ion supply chain is still immature, limiting near-term OEM deployment, but the chemistry warrants attention in design roadmaps for programs launching in 2028 and beyond.
Solid-State: Premium Performance at a Tariff Premium
Solid-state batteries – eliminating the liquid electrolyte in conventional lithium-ion cells – entered limited mass production in 2026 with manufacturers including Ganfeng Lithium reporting 500 Wh/kg energy density at small scale. For OEM applications in aerospace and defense where energy density and safety are paramount and cost is secondary, solid-state is a compelling roadmap consideration. However, the manufacturing supply chain for solid-state is almost entirely in China and Japan, meaning tariff exposure for US OEMs is significant. Most commercially viable solid-state OEM applications are 3 to 5 years from mainstream availability at acceptable price points.
NMC (Lithium Nickel Manganese Cobalt)
Highest energy density of mainstream chemistries. High tariff exposure via cobalt (DRC quotas) and nickel. Best for applications where energy density justifies premium cost: portable medical, UAV flight packs.
 
LFP (Lithium Iron Phosphate)
No cobalt, no nickel. Lowest tariff exposure. Excellent cycle life and thermal safety. Growing fast in industrial, stationary, and commercial applications. Flat discharge curve requires sophisticated BMS.
 
NiMH (Nickel Metal Hydride)
No lithium – fully insulated from lithium tariffs. Lower energy density. Still viable for specific medical device and industrial applications where lithium approval is difficult or where cycle life at low C-rates matters.
 
Sodium-Ion
No lithium, cobalt, or nickel. Maximum tariff insulation. Immature supply chain. Best suited for stationary and low-energy applications. Significant OEM opportunity in 2028 and beyond when supply chains mature.
 
Solid-State
Highest energy density potential. Chinese and Japanese manufacturing base creates tariff exposure for US OEMs. Premium pricing. Defense and aerospace applications where cost is secondary to performance are the near-term OEM target.
 
Silicon-Anode Li-Ion
Higher energy density than graphite-anode Li-Ion. First large-scale EV deployments in 2026. Eliminates most graphite tariff exposure. Premium cost but rapidly falling. Relevant for high-density OEM applications in aerospace and medical.
 
 
5. Sector-by-Sector Impact: Medical, Military, Industrial, and More
Tariff impacts vary significantly by sector, driven by differences in procurement rules, certification requirements, and the ability to pass cost increases to end customers. Here is how each of Totex’s core markets is experiencing the current environment.
Medical Devices
The medical device battery market faces a unique combination of tariff pressure and regulatory constraint. Medical OEMs cannot simply switch to a lower-tariff cell source without re-validating their device – an expensive, time-consuming process governed by FDA design control requirements. The cell used in a 510(k)-cleared or PMA-approved device is typically locked into the cleared submission. Switching requires at least a design change notification and often a new submission. This means medical OEMs are uniquely exposed to tariff-driven cost increases with limited ability to respond quickly.
The medium-term response for medical OEMs is to evaluate LFP as a platform chemistry for new designs where the energy density trade-off is acceptable. LFP’s safety profile – thermal runaway threshold above 500 degrees C versus approximately 200 degrees C for NMC – may actually simplify FDA submissions by reducing the safety justification burden. Totex’s engineering team has developed LFP-based designs for medical platforms where cycle life and safety are valued over maximum energy density.
Military and Aerospace
The aerospace and defense battery market has its own tariff story. Defense procurement regulations – particularly DFARS (Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement) clauses – already placed restrictions on Chinese-origin components in many defense programs. The new FEOC regulations in 2026 extended and codified these restrictions, creating a compliance framework that goes beyond cost into legal risk.
For defense OEMs, the tariff environment has accelerated a shift toward domestically manufactured battery packs with documented, traceable supply chains. This plays directly to Totex’s strengths: our California manufacturing facility, ISO 9001 and 13485 certifications, and MIL-STD-810 environmental testing capability position us as a compliant domestic supplier for defense programs requiring American-origin batteries. Solid-state and high-energy-density silicon-anode chemistries are increasingly relevant for UAV and soldier-portable applications where weight and runtime are mission-critical.
Industrial and Test and Measurement
The industrial battery market and test and measurement sector are experiencing tariff pressure but have more flexibility than medical to respond. Industrial OEMs are not locked into specific cells by regulatory submissions, so chemistry and sourcing changes can be implemented more quickly. The primary constraint is product qualification testing – vibration, drop, temperature cycling, and IP rating validation – which takes time and budget even when regulatory submissions are not required. Totex’s in-house environmental testing lab gives our customers an advantage here, enabling rapid re-qualification of alternative cell sources without outsourcing delays.
LFP is seeing strong adoption in industrial applications for exactly the reasons tariffs make it attractive: no cobalt or nickel exposure, low raw material cost per cycle, and long service life that reduces total cost of ownership. For industrial devices with 5 to 10-year service requirements, LFP’s 3,000 to 10,000 cycle life means the battery may outlast the device itself – a compelling economic argument regardless of tariff considerations.
Consumer OEM
The consumer OEM market faces the most direct tariff pressure because consumer products compete intensely on price and have the least ability to absorb cost increases through premium pricing. Consumer OEMs sourcing from Chinese manufacturers face the full stack of tariffs, while the competitive pricing of Chinese-manufactured end products in their markets is simultaneously being challenged by the same tariff environment. The result is margin compression from both directions. For consumer OEMs who have not yet explored domestic or nearshore manufacturing, the current environment may represent the tipping point where total landed cost analysis favors a different supply chain model.

7. Five Strategies OEMs Are Using Right Now
Based on conversations with procurement managers and program engineers across our customer base, here are the strategies that OEMs are deploying in response to the tariff environment in 2026.
Strategy 1: Full BOM Tariff Audit
The first step for any OEM that has not done this recently: audit the entire battery pack bill of materials for tariff exposure by component, by HTS code, and by country of origin. Many OEMs are surprised to find that their “Korean” battery pack contains Chinese graphite, Chinese BMS components, or Chinese-origin copper. A complete tariff audit often reveals that strategic substitution of two or three components can dramatically reduce total tariff exposure without requiring a full redesign.
Strategy 2: Chemistry Migration Planning
For OEMs whose current programs use NMC or NCA chemistries and are launching new product generations in 2026 or 2027, evaluating LFP as the platform chemistry for the new design is worth the engineering effort. The tariff savings, combined with LFP’s cycle life advantage, often justify the engineering investment in new BMS firmware and the product validation cycle. Totex’s engineering team can model the performance, cost, and regulatory implications of a chemistry migration for your specific application.
Strategy 3: Long-Term Supply Agreements
Tariff uncertainty creates strong incentives for longer-term supply agreements that lock in pricing before further tariff escalation. OEMs who signed multi-year battery supply agreements in early 2025, before the worst of the tariff increases took effect, are significantly better positioned than those buying spot. For new programs, discussing long-term pricing frameworks with battery suppliers is now a procurement priority rather than an afterthought.
Strategy 4: Dual-Source Qualification
Qualifying two battery suppliers – one domestic or nearshore, one offshore – gives OEMs flexibility to shift sourcing based on tariff developments without disrupting production. This requires upfront engineering investment to qualify both sources, but the supply chain resilience benefit increasingly justifies that investment. Totex can serve as either the primary or secondary source in a dual-source program, depending on the OEM’s preference.
Strategy 5: VMI and Consignment to Manage Tariff Timing
Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI) and consignment programs allow OEMs to shift inventory ownership – and therefore customs duty payment timing – to the supplier. In a tariff environment where policy can change between when inventory is ordered and when it is consumed, this timing flexibility has real economic value. Totex’s logistics and material planning team actively manages VMI and consignment programs for approximately 70 percent of our customer base.
8. FEOC Compliance: The Regulation That Changes Everything in 2026
The Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) regulations represent the most significant new compliance requirement for OEM battery supply chains in 2026. Unlike tariffs – which affect cost – FEOC violations affect legal compliance. For OEMs in defense, government, or federally funded research programs, FEOC non-compliance is not a cost problem; it is a contract performance problem.
What Is FEOC?
FEOC regulations, implemented under the Inflation Reduction Act and subsequent guidance, define certain Chinese, Russian, North Korean, and Iranian entities as “Foreign Entities of Concern” whose involvement in battery supply chains can disqualify products from federal incentives and, in some contexts, from federal procurement contracts. The regulations apply not just to final assembly but to battery materials – cells, cathode active materials, anode materials, electrolytes, and separators – across the supply chain.
Who Is Affected
FEOC compliance is most immediately critical for:
- Defense and aerospace OEMs with DFARS compliance requirements in government contracts
- Medical device OEMs with federal procurement contracts or federally funded programs
- OEMs seeking to qualify for remaining federal incentive programs that carry FEOC conditions
- Any OEM whose end customers include the US federal government or federally funded entities
The Documentation Requirement
FEOC compliance requires documentation that is far more granular than most battery suppliers have historically maintained. Demonstrating FEOC compliance means tracing not just where the battery was assembled, but where each material in the battery originated – including graphite, lithium carbonate, cathode active material, separator, and electrolyte. Totex’s quality management system is designed to maintain this documentation traceability for programs that require it. OEMs with FEOC-sensitive programs should raise this requirement explicitly at the beginning of any supplier engagement.

9. Ten Questions to Ask Your Battery Supplier About Tariff Exposure
If you have not had an explicit tariff and supply chain conversation with your battery supplier recently, these questions will help structure that discussion and reveal where your program is exposed.
- What is the country of origin for the cells in my battery pack, and what tariff classification applies?
A supplier who cannot answer this precisely does not have the supply chain visibility you need in 2026.
- Where does the graphite in the anode come from, and is it subject to the 93.5 percent Chinese graphite tariff?
Most Li-Ion cells globally use Chinese graphite. If your supplier says “Korea” or “Japan,” ask where those manufacturers source their graphite.
- Does your BMS use components of Chinese origin, and if so, what are the HTS codes and tariff rates?
BMS electronics sourced from China carry 25 to 30 percent tariffs that can meaningfully affect smart battery pack pricing.

10. 2026 Outlook: What Comes Next
Predicting the tariff environment with precision is not possible – policy changes can happen quickly, court decisions can invalidate existing measures, and geopolitical developments can reshape trade relationships in ways that no forecast captures. What we can do is identify the forces that are most likely to shape the battery sourcing environment through the remainder of 2026 and into 2027.
The Forces That Are Unlikely to Reverse
Some of what has changed in the battery supply chain is structural rather than political, and therefore less likely to reverse regardless of tariff policy developments.
- FEOC compliance requirements are now embedded in defense and federal procurement regulations independent of tariff policy
- US domestic battery manufacturing investment from the IRA era is in place and unlikely to reverse even as subsidies expire
- LFP chemistry adoption is a global market trend driven by cost and performance, not just tariff policy
- Supply chain transparency demands from defense and medical customers will continue regardless of tariff levels
The Forces That Could Change
- Tariff rates on specific materials could be reduced through trade negotiations or legal challenges
- New trade agreements could change the tariff treatment of battery components from specific countries
- Section 232 investigations could add new tariff measures not yet in place
- Chinese manufacturers continuing to expand Southeast Asian production could create new tariff-advantaged sourcing routes
