Importing Auto Parts into US: The 2026 Section 232 Playbook for Buyers, Dealers, and Distributors
The rules for importing auto parts into the US changed dramatically in May 2025, when a 25 percent Section 232 tariff hit engines, transmissions, powertrain components, and electrical parts — regardless of country of origin. Add the standard 2.5 percent base duty, USMCA content carve-outs, EPA and DOT compliance, and the reality that a single misclassified shipment can wipe out a quarter's margin, and you have one of the most complex commercial import categories in the country today. This playbook walks through every step of importing auto parts into US warehouses in 2026, with the current tariff numbers, the documents that matter, and the small details that decide whether your shipment clears cleanly or sits at the port.
The auto parts import market in 2026
The United States imports roughly $200 billion in auto parts annually, drawn from Mexico, Canada, China, Japan, Germany, South Korea, and a long tail of smaller suppliers. With more than 283 million registered vehicles on American roads and a healthy aftermarket sector, demand for everything from OEM replacement parts to performance upgrades remains consistently strong. For dealers, distributors, repair shops, and enthusiasts, importing the right parts at the right price can be the difference between a profitable operation and a struggling one.
The catch is that the rules tightened sharply in 2025. The Section 232 tariffs that took effect on May 3, 2025 represent the biggest single change to US auto parts import economics in a generation. Combine that with EPA emissions oversight on engines, DOT safety requirements on key components, NHTSA standards on anything road-going, and the standard HTSUS duty framework, and you have a category where compliance discipline directly determines whether the business model works.
The good news is that the path is well-defined. The rules are public, the agencies have published guidance, and the importers who understand the system can still operate profitably. The buyers who lose money are the ones who assume the rules from three years ago still apply or that "we have always done it this way" is a compliance strategy. It is not.
Section 232: the rule that changed everything
Before we go anywhere else, let's deal with the elephant in the room. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 allows the President to impose tariffs on imports that threaten national security. In March 2025, the administration invoked Section 232 to impose a 25 percent ad valorem tariff on imported passenger vehicles and certain auto parts. The parts tariff took effect on May 3, 2025, and it applies regardless of country of origin — with one important exception we'll get to in a moment.
What Section 232 covers for auto parts
The 25 percent tariff applies to auto parts for passenger vehicles and light trucks, specifically including:
- Engines and engine parts
- Transmissions
- Powertrain components (driveshafts, axles, differentials)
- Electrical components
- Additional parts may be added through subsequent rulemaking
Parts that fall outside this list — body panels, interior trim, basic accessories — continue under their pre-existing duty rates, which for most auto parts is around 2.5 percent. But for the high-value categories that drive most aftermarket and OEM imports, the new 25 percent rate is now the headline cost line.
The USMCA carve-out you must understand
Auto parts that qualify under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) get treated differently. During the transition period that began with the proclamation, USMCA-qualifying parts from Canada and Mexico remain tariff-free. Once the Secretary of Commerce, in consultation with CBP, establishes the system to apply the tariff only to the non-US content of qualifying parts, USMCA imports will be tariffed on that non-US value rather than the full part value.
This is a unique structure — most tariff regimes don't compute on partial value — and it rewards manufacturers who can document substantial US content in their parts. It is also one of the reasons sourcing decisions for the next several years are pivoting toward Mexican and Canadian production for the US market.
The Section 232 auto parts tariff is not subject to duty drawback. That means if you import parts, pay the 25 percent, and later re-export them or use them in re-exported goods, you cannot recover the tariff. This rules out a common cost-mitigation strategy used in other tariff regimes and significantly affects business models that previously relied on bonded operations or duty drawback recovery.
The 8-step import process
Every successful auto parts import follows the same basic sequence. Knowing each step helps you see where things can stall and where you can move faster.
Get the exact code; this controls duty plus Section 232.
Check origin and rules of origin if from Canada or Mexico.
Determine if HS-7, 3520-1, or other forms apply.
Customs broker, bond, EIN, POA, ISF filing.
Ocean, air, or truck depending on size and timing.
Submit entry in ACE before vessel arrival.
Settle base duty, Section 232, MPF, HMF.
Final transport to warehouse; retain records 5 years.
From the moment you commit to a purchase order to the moment the parts hit your warehouse shelf, plan for 35 to 70 days for a typical ocean shipment, or 7 to 15 days for air freight. Section 232 compliance has not slowed the actual clearance step — clean entries still clear in 1 to 3 days at most major ports — but it has materially raised the cost line that you settle at clearance.
Auto parts categories and what each one triggers
Different parts categories trigger different regulatory layers. Understanding which forms and agencies apply to your specific items helps you plan documentation and avoid mid-shipment surprises.
| Parts category | Section 232 | EPA / DOT triggers | 
|---|---|---|
| Engines & engine parts | Yes (25%) | EPA Form 3520-1; emissions standard required | 
| Transmissions & gearboxes | Yes (25%) | Limited; mostly driveline | 
| Driveshafts, axles, differentials | Yes (25%) | Limited | 
| Electrical components (alternators, starters, ECUs) | Yes (25%) | FCC if radio-frequency; EPA if emissions-related | 
| Brake components | Limited Section 232 | DOT HS-7 for road-going safety items | 
| Suspension & steering | Limited | DOT HS-7 where applicable | 
| Body panels, fenders, hoods | No | Limited; mostly cosmetic | 
| Lighting (headlights, taillights) | No | DOT FMVSS 108 compliance required | 
| Tires | No | DOT tire identification; FMVSS 109/119 | 
| Wheels & rims | No (steel may face 232 on steel) | Limited | 
| Interior trim & upholstery | No | Limited | 
| Catalytic converters | Limited | EPA emissions critical; theft-prevention rules | 
| Aftermarket performance parts | Depends on classification | EPA for emissions-affecting; CARB for California | 
Two practical rules from this table. First, the highest-value parts (engines, transmissions, electronics) carry the highest compliance burden — Section 232 plus EPA plus potential DOT. Second, parts that don't affect emissions or safety often have the simplest path through customs, even if they fall outside Section 232. Aftermarket cosmetic accessories are far easier to import than a performance ECU upgrade.
Federal agencies you must satisfy
Auto parts imports pass through several federal regulators in addition to customs. Each one has its own forms, its own concerns, and its own authority to halt a shipment.
US Customs and Border Protection (CBP)
The gatekeeper. CBP reviews every commercial entry, classifies the goods against the HTSUS, assesses duties (including Section 232 where applicable), and either releases the shipment or holds it for examination. Every auto parts import passes through CBP, and the customs entry is the central administrative event of the import.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
EPA enforces the Clean Air Act for emissions-related parts. Imported engines, catalytic converters, fuel system components, and emissions control devices typically require EPA Form 3520-1 declaring compliance with applicable emissions standards. EPA enforcement on imported engines has intensified through 2025 and 2026, particularly on uncertified JDM engines and aftermarket performance parts that defeat emissions controls.
National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)
NHTSA, operating under the Department of Transportation, enforces Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) for parts that affect vehicle safety. Brake components, tires, lighting (FMVSS 108), occupant restraint parts, and certain glazing materials all must meet specific FMVSS requirements. Non-compliant parts can be refused at the border or recalled later.
Department of Transportation (DOT)
For road-going parts subject to FMVSS, importers file DOT Form HS-7 declaring compliance with applicable standards. Parts that meet recognized FMVSS get a check mark; parts that don't may face Independent Commercial Importer modification or refusal.
Federal Communications Commission (FCC)
Electronic auto parts with radio-frequency components — wireless ECUs, key fob systems, telematics modules — require FCC certification. Most major-brand parts already carry FCC IDs; aftermarket and gray-market electronics often don't, and shipments without proper certification can be seized.
California Air Resources Board (CARB)
Technically a state-level agency, but it functions like a federal one for parts destined for California. CARB Executive Order numbers are required on emissions-affecting aftermarket parts sold in California, and many distributors require CARB approval before they will stock a part. Parts that lack CARB approval can be imported into the US but cannot legally be sold or installed on California-registered vehicles.

Documents every auto parts shipment needs
Documentation is the single biggest determinant of whether your shipment clears smoothly or sits at the port. Missing or inconsistent paperwork is the most common cause of preventable delays in auto parts imports.
- Commercial invoice — describes the parts, value, buyer, seller, and terms of sale
- Packing list — itemizes contents, weights, and dimensions of each carton
- Bill of lading or air waybill — the carrier's contract and receipt for the cargo
- Customs bond — required for shipments valued at $2,500 or more
- Importer Security Filing (ISF / 10+2) — submitted 24 hours before ocean vessel loading
- USMCA Certification of Origin — required to claim USMCA treatment on Canada/Mexico imports
- Country of origin marking documentation — proof of the manufacturing country
- EPA Form 3520-1 — for emissions-related engines and components
- DOT Form HS-7 — for parts subject to FMVSS
- Power of attorney — authorizing your customs broker to file on your behalf
- EIN (Employer Identification Number) — required for any commercial importer
- Manufacturer certifications — CARB EO, FCC ID, ECE/SAE markings where applicable
The single most important consistency check is that the commercial invoice, packing list, and ISF all match. When the value declared on the invoice differs from the value in your filed customs entry, CBP holds the shipment for verification, and the resolution process can take days.

HTS classification and why it controls your bill
The Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS) is the master classification system that assigns every imported good a 10-digit code. For auto parts, the code determines the base duty rate, whether Section 232 applies, what EPA/DOT forms are required, and whether USMCA preferences are available. Misclassification is the single fastest way to either overpay duty for years or get hit with a back-duty assessment when CBP audits the entry.
Common auto parts HTS codes
Most auto parts sit under Chapter 87 of the HTSUS, covering vehicles and parts thereof. Engines sit under Chapter 84 (machinery). Electrical components, ECUs, and electronics often classify under Chapter 85. Knowing the right chapter is the start. The exact 10-digit code matters because two parts that look identical can carry materially different duty rates depending on classification.
Three rules for getting classification right
- Classify before you negotiate. Know the HTS code (and therefore the landed cost) before agreeing to a purchase price with your supplier. Many first-time importers calculate margin against the supplier's quote and discover at clearance that the real cost is 25 to 35 percent higher.
- Don't rely on the supplier's classification. Foreign suppliers often quote HS codes that are correct for their export country but wrong for the US. The 10-digit US classification is your responsibility.
- Get a binding ruling for high-volume parts. CBP offers binding rulings that lock in the classification for future shipments. For parts you import repeatedly, a binding ruling is cheap insurance against reclassification disputes.

USMCA: the only meaningful tariff exemption
For most auto parts importers in 2026, USMCA qualification is the difference between a workable business model and an unprofitable one. The agreement provides duty-free treatment for qualifying parts from Canada and Mexico, and during the current transition period it shields qualifying parts from the full Section 232 25 percent tariff.
How parts qualify under USMCA
To qualify, an auto part must meet the USMCA Rules of Origin, which generally require:
- A specific Regional Value Content (RVC) percentage — typically 60 to 75 percent for auto parts depending on category and calculation method
- For "core" auto parts (engines, transmissions, batteries, etc.), additional Labor Value Content (LVC) requirements — minimum 30 to 45 percent of wages paid at $16/hour or more
- Steel and aluminum content sourcing requirements — minimum percentages from North America
- A valid USMCA Certification of Origin signed by the producer, exporter, or importer
The unusual non-US content rule
The Section 232 proclamation includes a unique provision for USMCA-qualifying auto parts. Once the Commerce Department, in consultation with CBP, establishes the implementing process, the 25 percent tariff will apply only to the value of the non-US content of qualifying parts. A USMCA-qualifying part that is 70 percent US content would face the tariff only on the 30 percent non-US portion. This rewards genuine North American manufacturing depth and incentivizes auto suppliers to deepen US content in their products.
Until the implementing process is fully operational, USMCA-qualifying parts remain exempt during the transition period. Importers should not assume this will continue indefinitely — once the system is in place, USMCA documentation will need to support the specific US-content claim.
Even if your parts come from a USMCA country, you do not automatically qualify for preferential treatment. The Certification of Origin and supporting rules of origin documentation must be in hand at the time of entry. Importers who simply assume "Mexican origin equals USMCA-qualifying" frequently get caught when CBP asks for the supporting documentation and it doesn't exist.
Real 2026 cost breakdown
Numbers make the abstract concrete. Here is a worked example: a commercial import of 100 replacement engines from a Japanese OEM supplier (declared value $400,000) arriving at the Port of Long Beach in 2026. Engines fall squarely within the Section 232 scope.
| Cost line | Amount (USD) | Notes | 
|---|---|---|
| Product cost (100 engines × $4,000) | $400,000 | FOB Yokohama supplier pricing | 
| Inland Japanese transport | $1,800 | Supplier to Yokohama port | 
| Export documentation | $250 | Commercial invoice, packing list, B/L | 
| Ocean freight (40ft container, Yokohama → Long Beach) | $4,200 | ~14-day transit in 2026 | 
| Cargo insurance (0.5%) | $2,000 | Standard high-value freight coverage | 
| Base import duty on engines (HTS 8407) | $10,000 | 2.5% base rate on $400,000 | 
| Section 232 tariff (25%) | $100,000 | Applies to full value, no drawback | 
| Merchandise Processing Fee (capped) | $634 | 0.3464% capped at 2026 maximum | 
| Harbor Maintenance Fee | $500 | 0.125% on entered value | 
| EPA Form 3520-1 filing | Included | Filed with broker entry | 
| Customs broker fee | $425 | Auto-parts experienced broker | 
| Customs bond (continuous, prorated) | $120 | Annual bond cost prorated | 
| Container Freight Station (LA/LB) | $580 | Port handling, chassis | 
| Drayage (port to LA warehouse) | $680 | Within Southern California | 
| Container unload labor | $340 | 2-3 hours at warehouse | 
| Total landed cost | $521,527 | $5,215 per engine, +30% over FOB | 
Look at the math. The supplier quoted $4,000 per engine. The real landed cost is $5,215 per engine, more than 30 percent higher. The single largest line is the Section 232 tariff at $100,000 — almost three times the base duty. If you priced your wholesale at $5,800 expecting a $1,800 gross margin, your actual gross margin is closer to $585 before any platform fees, returns, or storage. That gap is exactly why so many auto parts importers got caught in the second half of 2025 when the Section 232 tariff fully took effect.
EPA testing fees if your engine isn't pre-certified ($1,500 to $8,000 per model). Independent Commercial Importer fees for non-conforming engines ($3,000 to $15,000 per unit). CARB approval for California sales ($30,000 to $80,000 per part family, but covers unlimited units). Demurrage at busy ports during peak season ($150 to $300 per day). Always build a 5 to 10 percent contingency into your landed cost.

Personal use vs commercial resale
The rules differ meaningfully depending on whether you are bringing in parts for your own vehicle or buying them to resell. Knowing which category you fall under shapes the paperwork, the bond requirements, and your ongoing compliance burden.
What applies
- Smaller-scale, often single shipments
- SSN can serve as importer number
- No customs bond if under $2,500
- Still pay applicable duties + Section 232
- EPA / DOT compliance still required for emissions/safety parts
- No commercial recordkeeping burden
- Restricted to parts for personal vehicle
What applies
- Recurring shipments, larger volume
- EIN as importer of record number
- Continuous customs bond required
- All duties + Section 232 apply
- Full EPA / DOT compliance program
- 5-year recordkeeping requirement
- Subject to periodic CBP audits
For enthusiasts importing engines, transmissions, or specialty parts for their own vehicle, the personal-use route is significantly simpler — but the tariff math is the same. If you are restoring or modifying a sports car with imported parts, the principles for vehicle imports apply directly to the parts side as well, especially around EPA emissions, DOT safety compliance, and the 25-Year Rule implications when sourcing parts for vehicles in that age range.

Mistakes that delay or block auto parts shipments
Most disappointing auto parts imports trace back to the same handful of avoidable mistakes. Avoid these and you will be ahead of most first-time importers.
- Ignoring Section 232 in your pricing. Calculating margin against the 2.5 percent base duty alone is the most expensive mistake in 2026 auto parts importing. The real number for covered parts is 27.5 percent or higher.
- Trusting the supplier's HS code. Foreign HS codes don't always map cleanly to US HTS codes. Get the US 10-digit classification verified before you commit to a purchase.
- Skipping USMCA Certification of Origin. Buying from Mexico or Canada doesn't automatically get you USMCA treatment. Without proper documentation, you pay the full tariff.
- Missing EPA Form 3520-1. Importing engines or emissions-related parts without the EPA filing is one of the fastest ways to get a shipment seized.
- Buying uncertified JDM engines for road use. Modern road-going vehicles need EPA-certified engines. A non-certified JDM swap can clear customs but cannot legally be registered for road use in most states.
- No customs bond on commercial shipments. Anything valued $2,500 or more needs a bond. Without it, CBP holds the cargo.
- Underdeclaring value to reduce duty. This is customs fraud, not a cost-saving strategy. Penalties dwarf any duty saved, and the importer's record gets flagged for future scrutiny.
- Forgetting CARB approval for California sales. A part that's federally legal can still be illegal to install on a California-registered vehicle without CARB Executive Order approval.
- Generic broker for specialty parts. Auto parts have specific compliance layers (EPA, DOT, FCC, CARB). Use a broker with documented auto parts experience.
- Importing from a sanctioned source. Always screen suppliers against denied party lists, even for routine commercial parts. Sanctions enforcement applies to auto parts the same as anything else.
