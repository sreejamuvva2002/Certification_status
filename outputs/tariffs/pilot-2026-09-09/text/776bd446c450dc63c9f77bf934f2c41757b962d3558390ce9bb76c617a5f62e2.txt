Mexico and the European Union are set to eliminate mutual tariffs on electric vehicles and batteries as part of a modernized trade framework designed to insulate both economies from shifting North American trade policies and strengthen bilateral industrial ties.
The Integrated Trade Agreement (ITA), a core component of the broader Modernized Global Agreement (MGA), is scheduled to be signed this Friday. The pact aims to liberalize 94% of trade between the two parties, addressing sectors that did not exist when the original Free Trade Agreement between Mexico and the European Union (EU-Mexico FTA) entered into force 25 years ago.
“The most obvious opportunities are a reduction in tariffs; that is to say, it is going to be much cheaper to export practically all agricultural products, manufacturing, and the automotive industry,” said Marcelo Ebrard, Mexico’s Minister of Economy, during the Mexico–European Union Business Summit. “This opens new possibilities for us in a global trend that is moving in the opposite direction.”
Addressing the Electric Vehicle Gap
The original trade agreement, which entered into force in July 2000, phased out tariffs on nearly all industrial products by 2007. However, the rapid technological evolution of the automotive sector left newer categories—specifically electric vehicles (EVs) and lithium-ion batteries—outside the original duty-free scope.
Currently, Mexican exports of vehicles and parts to the EU face an average tariff of approximately 10%. The new agreement explicitly includes these sectors to reflect the current industrial landscape. Rosendo Gutiérrez, Mexico’s Deputy Secretary of Foreign Trade, said the removal of these duties will boost bilateral automotive trade and provide greater legal certainty for long-term investments.
The timing of the agreement coincides with a sharp downturn in Mexico’s automotive trade with the United States. According to US Department of Commerce data, Mexican EV exports to the United States fell 69.6% year on year in 1Q26, totaling US$700 million. Total automotive exports from Mexico to the United States, including trucks and parts, dropped 11.3% over the same period to US$38.05 billion. This follows a 7.2% decline recorded throughout 2025.
Strategic Diversification Amid US Tariffs
The deal is viewed by officials as a strategic pivot as the administration of US President Donald Trump maintains a 25% tariff on vehicles and parts that fail to meet strict rules of origin under the USMCA.
“Mexico wants to reduce its dependence on its northern neighbor, but also on Asian, or rather Chinese, supply chains, and in Europe we are pursuing the same objectives,” an EU official said on condition of anonymity.
Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum underscored the need to “open other horizons” as both Mexico and the EU navigate a more protectionist US trade environment. While the United States has recently set tariffs on most European goods at 15%, Mexico’s average tariffs to the United States remain lower under the USMCA, positioning Mexico as a potential bridge between the two markets.
“Mexico will be the point of convergence, the platform for the European Union and North America to come together,” said Sergio Contreras, president, Mexican Business Council for Foreign Trade.
Investment Trends and ImplementationEl
The European Union is currently Mexico’s third-largest trading partner, following the United States and China. Despite recent volatility, the EU–Mexico commercial relationship has grown 75% over the past decade.
In 2025, Mexico’s automotive sector attracted investment across 204 projects from 20 countries. The United States accounted for 45% of these projects, followed by Japan at 12% and Germany at 10%. Rogelio Garza, Executive President, AMIA, highlighted a shift toward domestic stability, noting that reinvestment by existing firms represented 53.4% of total foreign direct investment (FDI) in the sector.
“We are practically going to eliminate all tariffs to be able to access the European market. Basically, we will have everything related to the export of cars and auto parts without tariffs,” Ebrard said.
The agreement is scheduled to be signed this Friday during the eighth EU–Mexico Summit, attended by President Sheinbaum and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. Following the signature, the ITA must be ratified by the European Parliament and the Mexican Senate. Commissioner Šefčovič expressed confidence that the agreement would enter into full force before the end of 2026.
