How tariff-driven steel and aluminum price spikes are reshaping the cost structure of wind turbines and electric vehicles, forcing OEMs to re-engineer supply chains and pass through costs.
Source: US International Trade Commission (USITC) & ESI Analysis
Wind turbine towers require heavy steel plates (ASTM A572 Grade 50) and hot-rolled coils. Domestic mills operate at ~85% capacity, and lead times for plate have extended to 12-16 weeks. Tariffs have reduced import competition, giving domestic mills pricing power. OEMs face a 25% cost premium on imported steel, but domestic steel is also priced higher due to reduced supply.
EV battery enclosures and structural components rely on aluminum extrusions and high-pressure die castings. The 10% tariff on aluminum imports has increased costs for extrusions by 8-12%, as domestic smelters cannot meet demand. OEMs are forced to source from a limited number of US-based extruders, creating a bottleneck for EV production ramp-ups.
The exclusion process for Section 232 is complex and time-consuming, with approval rates around 30%. OEMs must prove that no US-produced alternative exists. This creates uncertainty and delays, forcing companies to either absorb costs or pass them to consumers. The process is a critical chokepoint for supply chain planning.

The Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) constitute a structural cost shock and supply-chain distortion for US wind and EV manufacturing. Steel represents 70–80% of turbine tower and nacelle mass, while steel and aluminum account for 50–60% of EV body and chassis weight. The tariffs have added $15–20/kW to turbine costs and $800–1,200 per EV, directly compressing OEM margins and raising project capital expenditure. Critically, the tariffs have created a two-tier domestic market: US-produced steel and aluminum command a premium due to limited capacity and import substitution, while imported materials face punitive duties, forcing OEMs to either absorb costs or pass them downstream. This bifurcation introduces single-point-of-failure vulnerabilities—domestic mills are operating near capacity, and any disruption (labor strike, natural disaster) would cascade into severe supply shortages. Furthermore, the tariff regime has accelerated contract renegotiations, with OEMs seeking long-term fixed-price agreements and domestic content commitments, but the lack of exclusions for critical components (e.g., specialty alloys, forgings) leaves residual cost exposure. The systemic risk is amplified by the potential for retaliatory tariffs on US exports, which could erode the competitiveness of US-made turbines and EVs in global markets. Policy uncertainty—whether tariffs persist, escalate, or are replaced by quotas—creates a volatile planning environment, deterring capital investment in new manufacturing capacity. Strategic responses include reshoring supply chains, qualifying alternative materials, and lobbying for targeted exclusions, but these measures take years to yield cost relief. In the interim, the tariffs act as a regressive tax on the energy transition, increasing the levelized cost of wind and EV adoption, and potentially slowing the pace of decarbonization in the US.
