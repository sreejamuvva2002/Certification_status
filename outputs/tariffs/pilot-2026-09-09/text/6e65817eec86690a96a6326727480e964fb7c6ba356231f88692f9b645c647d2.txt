TL;DR
The intercompany price on a multinational's own goods is scored by two agencies with opposite incentives, the IRS wants it high enough to keep US taxable income honest and CBP wants it defensible as the basis for duty, and post-importation transfer pricing adjustments only reach customs value if the importer met all five conditions of CBP's HQ W548314 test before the goods arrived.
Does transfer pricing affect customs value?
Directly, and usually without anyone deciding that it should. When a multinational moves goods between its own entities, the intercompany transfer price is typically what gets declared as transaction value under 19 U.S.C. 1401a, so a transfer pricing policy written for tax purposes silently becomes the basis for duty. Two agencies then score the same number with opposite incentives.
The Two-Ledger Price problem: one intercompany price, two federal agencies, opposite pressures. The IRS scrutinizes prices set low enough to shift income out of the U.S.; CBP scrutinizes prices set low enough to shrink the duty base. Practitioners have called the result a whip-saw for years, and stacked tariffs made it expensive, the July 2026 Section 301 forced-labor action added 10 or 12.5 percent on top of MFN across the top 60 trade partners, which USTR's fact sheet states covers 99.4 percent of U.S. imports. Every point of transfer price is now a duty decision.

When does CBP accept a related-party transfer price?
When the relationship demonstrably did not influence the price. Under 19 U.S.C. 1401a, a related-party transaction value is acceptable if either the circumstances of sale show the price was settled the way it would be between unrelated parties, or the value closely approximates recognized test values. Neither test is satisfied by a transfer pricing study alone, because a section 482 study answers a different question, whether the price is arm's length for income allocation, not whether the relationship influenced this particular sale.
What CBP actually examines in the circumstances of sale test
The governing standard in 19 CFR 152.103 is plainer than its reputation: "If it is shown that the buyer and seller, although related, buy from and sell to each other as if they were not related, this will demonstrate that the price has not been influenced by the relationship, and the transaction value will be accepted." The examination is case-by-case, and three showings carry most of the weight:
- Industry practice. The price was settled in a manner consistent with the normal pricing practices of the industry in question.
- Consistency with unrelated sales. The price was settled in the way the seller settles prices with buyers who are not related to it.
- Cost recovery plus profit. The price is adequate to ensure recovery of all costs plus a profit equivalent to the firm's overall profit realized over a representative period of time.
The third is the one that most often decides a related-party file, because it is the closest analogue to a transfer pricing analysis, and also the point where the two disciplines quietly diverge: a section 482 study may target a tested-party margin on a routine-function distributor, while CBP asks whether this price recovers the seller's costs plus its own overall profit. Two defensible answers, one number, and the importer holds whichever documentation it built.
What are test values, and when do they help?
The alternative route to acceptability. Under 19 U.S.C. 1401a, a related-party transaction value is also acceptable when it closely approximates one of these, occurring at or about the same time:
| Test value | What it is | Practical availability | 
|---|---|---|
| Transaction value of identical or similar merchandise | Sales of comparable goods to unrelated U.S. buyers | Strong when the group also sells to third parties | 
| Deductive value of identical or similar merchandise | Derived from the U.S. resale price, less permitted deductions | Workable for distribution models with clean resale data | 
| Computed value of identical or similar merchandise | Materials, processing, profit, and general expenses | Fits manufacturers who already build cost stacks | 
Test values are comparisons for acceptability, not substitutes for your declared value, and the regulation is explicit on timing: each comparison value must relate to merchandise "that was exported to the United States at or about the same time as the imported merchandise." Manufacturers with genuinely captive supply chains often find no usable test value exists, which pushes the whole file back onto the circumstances-of-sale route and makes the documentation there the only defense.
That gap is where most exposure sits. A manufacturer with several importing entities can hold a defensible TP study, file entries at exactly the study's price, and still lack the customs-side documentation CBP asks for, because nobody ever translated the tax file into a customs file. Can you adjust customs value after importation?
Yes, if you set it up beforehand. CBP's HQ W548314, issued May 16, 2012 and titled Transaction Value; Formulas; Post-Importation Adjustments; Revocation of HRL 547654, allows post-importation transfer pricing adjustments, both upward and downward, to be treated as part of transaction value when formula pricing meets all five of these conditions:
- A written TP policy in place before importation, prepared taking IRC section 482 into account
- The taxpayer actually uses that policy in filing its income tax return, and reports adjustments arising from it
- The policy specifies how the price and any adjustments are determined for all covered products
- The company maintains and provides supporting accounting detail from its books and financial statements
- No other conditions exist that would prevent CBP from accepting the transfer price
The same ruling removed an earlier obstacle: under prior policy in HRL 547654, importers had to participate in CBP's Reconciliation program to make these adjustments. W548314 revoked that ruling, and its own title says so.
The Adjustment Asymmetry: the five-factor gate is not symmetric in its consequences. An upward year-end true-up on goods already imported raises the duty you owed, and knowing about it triggers the same reasonable-care obligations as any other underpayment under 19 U.S.C. 1484. A downward adjustment only becomes refundable if the policy and documentation existed before the goods arrived. Companies that skip the preconditions therefore inherit the liabilities and forfeit the refunds, which is the worst available position and the most common one.
What a year-end true-up looks like in duty terms
A worked case makes the asymmetry concrete. Assume a U.S. subsidiary of a manufacturer imports $60 million of finished goods a year from its parent, entered at the transfer price, with a blended duty rate of 9 percent after the current stack. At year-end the group's TP model calls for a 4 percent upward adjustment to land the U.S. entity in its target margin.
- The tax side books the adjustment and files accordingly, roughly $2.4 million of additional intercompany cost.
- The customs side now owes duty on value that was never declared: 9 percent of $2.4 million, about $216,000, plus the interest and reasonable-care exposure that attaches once the company knows.
- Reverse the direction and the mirror does not hold: a 4 percent downward adjustment is only refundable if the five-factor conditions were satisfied before importation. Without them, the same $216,000 simply stays with the government.
Nothing in that example requires anyone to have done anything wrong. It requires only that the TP calendar and the entry calendar never met, which is the default state at most groups. Scale it across several importing entities and the number becomes a board-level item rather than a compliance footnote, which is exactly how multi-entity duty reporting surfaces it.
What triggers a related-party valuation inquiry?
Four common triggers, roughly in order of how often they start the conversation:
- A CBP Form 28 request for information on entered value, often after a data anomaly across related-party entries
- A Focused Assessment where valuation is in scope, which pulls the TP policy into the audit file whether or not trade prepared it
- A pattern in the data itself, identical goods entered at materially different values across your own entities, the Same-SKU Spread problem in valuation form
- Your own disclosure, when a true-up is reported correctly and CBP asks how the underlying price was set
The defensible posture is the same in all four: a written policy that predates importation, entries that match it, and a reconciliation showing the two agree. CBP's Reconciliation program exists precisely because some value elements are unknowable at entry, and importers can flag entries for later adjustment; W548314 removed the requirement to use it for TP adjustments, but for companies with recurring true-ups it remains a clean mechanism rather than a burden. Check CBP's current guidance for flagging deadlines before relying on it.
Who owns the reconciliation between tax and customs?
Nobody, at most multinationals, which is precisely why audits find it. Tax owns the policy, trade or the broker owns the declaration, and the two sit in different systems on different calendars: the TP true-up happens at year-end close, the entries were filed daily throughout the year, and no standing process compares them. That is the same structural gap behind every other flavor of duty leakage, the five systems that disagree, showing up in its most expensive form because both a tax authority and a customs authority can audit the answer.
A practical division: tax keeps the policy and the section 482 defense; trade keeps classification, origin, and the declaration; finance owns the monthly reconciliation of declared entry values against intercompany invoices and the TP model, with adjustments routed to the right channel while windows are open, Post-Summary Correction or protest depending on liquidation status. For groups running several importing entities, the multi-entity duty ledger is where that reconciliation actually lives.

Frequently Asked Questions
- Does transfer pricing affect customs value?
- Directly. For related-party imports, the transfer price is usually the declared transaction value under 19 U.S.C. 1401a, so a transfer pricing policy sets the duty base whether or not tax and trade teams ever speak. CBP accepts a related-party price when the circumstances of sale show the relationship did not influence it, or when it closely approximates recognized test values. For a manufacturer moving intercompany goods across several entities, that means the TP study is a customs document nobody labeled as one.
- Can you adjust customs value after importation when transfer prices change?
- Yes, but only if the groundwork existed first. CBP's ruling HQ W548314 (May 16, 2012) permits post-importation adjustments, upward and downward, to enter transaction value when five conditions are all met, including a written transfer pricing policy prepared with IRS section 482 in mind and in place before importation. Miss the preconditions and upward adjustments still create exposure while downward adjustments buy you nothing. The asymmetry is the whole risk.
- What is the five-factor test in HQ W548314?
- Five conditions that must all hold for formula pricing to support post-importation adjustments: a written TP policy in place before importation and prepared taking IRC section 482 into account; the taxpayer actually uses that policy in its income tax return and reports resulting adjustments; the policy specifies how the price and adjustments are determined for all covered products; the company maintains and provides supporting accounting detail from its books; and no other conditions exist that would prevent CBP from accepting the transfer price. HQ W548314 also removed the prior requirement, under HRL 547654, to join the Reconciliation program to make such adjustments.
- Who should own transfer pricing and customs reconciliation?
- Tax owns the policy, trade owns the declaration, and the reconciliation between them belongs to finance, because it is the only function that sees both ledgers.
