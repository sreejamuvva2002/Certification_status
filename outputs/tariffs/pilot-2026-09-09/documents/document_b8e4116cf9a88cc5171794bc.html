Note that in the 'Aftermarket' services, 60% of cost are related to direct and indirect labor, so parts generate 40% of costs. Then let us assume that imported parts constitute 30% of the total parts Aftermarket cost. Let us assume the tariff is 20% So, 40%*30%*20%=2.4% total weighted increased cost for parts. Materially less than the 9% increase we experienced due to inflation in the very recent past, so the angst of tariffs are in my opinion overblown. Note, my calculation above may not be precise, but the point is that ALL tariffs must be put into context as a weighted impact. Then there is the area of remanufactured/recertified used parts that can be sourced in the US that are like-kind to new for often the same price or less as imported new.

    Starting November 1, the U.S. will impose a 25% tariff on imported medium and heavy-duty trucks. Last year, the U.S. imported over 340,000 trucks from Mexico, nearly triple 2019 levels, and half of their parts were U.S.-made. Now, the same trucks will be taxed as foreign. That’s not just an automaker problem. That’s a logistics problem. Because 73% of America’s freight moves by truck. Every cost hike here, parts, imports, maintenance, eventually lands on the invoice of someone trying to move goods. And ripple effects move fast: • Fleet leasing costs could jump 10–15%. • Smaller carriers will get squeezed hardest. • Freight rates could rise before the holiday season, right when inventory builds peak. Trade protection starts with good intentions, builds locally, creates jobs, but if supply chain math doesn’t add up, you end up with inflation in disguise. 👉 Do you think tariffs like this make American logistics stronger or just more expensive to run? #supplychain #logistics #manufacturing #trade #economy
