Customs Valuation [Royalties] in Contract Manufacturing – Key Takeaways A recent India CESTAT ruling (Customs Appeal No. 40085/2024) provides important clarity on when royalties paid by a beneficial owner become dutiable, even if the importer of record is a contract manufacturer. What happened? Xiaomi India paid royalties/licence fees to Qualcomm and Beijing Xiaomi for technology embedded in imported components and finished goods. While many goods were manufactured by Indian contract manufacturers, Xiaomi China controlled the import of components and the manufacturers could only sell their output to Xiaomi India. Revenue argued—and CESTAT agreed—that: The IP was embedded in the imported goods. Non-payment of royalties would halt imports entirely. Royalties were therefore a condition of sale and must be included in the customs assessable value. Xiaomi India, though not the importer of record, acted as the beneficial owner and was liable for valuation impacts. Why it matters: Royalty payments linked to the right to import can trigger customs duty—even if paid by a party other than the importer. Beneficial ownership extends beyond legal title to include control, benefit, and contractual influence. Contract manufacturing and IP licensing structures must be carefully reviewed for customs implications. An important reminder for companies using contract manufacturers and relying on third-party IP: valuation risk doesn’t end with the importer of record—it follows the economic reality of the transaction. As this is a recent ruling, it carries significant industry implications—and we can expect the matter to be appealed to the Supreme Court. https://lnkd.in/e8GUAXBr

    📱 CESTAT Chennai on Customs Valuation, Royalties & Beneficial Ownership – Xiaomi Case 📌 Case: Xiaomi Technology India Pvt. Ltd. & Ors. v. Principal Commissioner of Customs 📌 Tribunal: Customs, Excise & Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai 📌 Bench: Shri P. Dinesha (Judicial) & Shri M. Ajit Kumar (Technical) 📌 Date of Decision: 14 November 2025 🧾 Background DRI Investigation: Alleged Xiaomi India evaded customs duty by not including royalty/licence fees paid to Qualcomm and Beijing Xiaomi in the assessable value of imported phones/components. Contract Manufacturers: Imports routed through Foxconn/Rising Star, Flextronics, Hipad, DBG. Commissioner’s Order (Oct 2023): Redetermined assessable value, demanded differential duty, ordered confiscation, imposed penalties. Appeals: Xiaomi India & manufacturers challenged; Revenue also appealed against dropped demands/penalties. ⚖️ Tribunal’s Key Findings 1️⃣ Royalty & Licence Fees: Payments to Qualcomm (3.25%–5% of net selling price) and Beijing Xiaomi (2% of revenue) were directly linked to imported goods. Under Section 14 Customs Act & Rule 10 Valuation Rules, such royalties are includible in assessable value. 2️⃣ Suppression of Facts: Xiaomi India failed to disclose agreements to SVB until DRI investigation. Tribunal noted suppression and misstatement. 3️⃣ Beneficial Ownership Principle: Even if imports were made by contract manufacturers, Xiaomi India was the real economic beneficiary: It dictated supply chain terms. It paid royalties/licence fees tied to imported goods. It marketed and sold the finished phones in India. Tribunal pierced the corporate veil, holding Xiaomi India as the beneficial owner of imports, liable for customs duty on enhanced value. 4️⃣ Burden of Proof: Customs must establish undervaluation; once prima facie case shown, onus shifts to importer. Standard of proof: preponderance of probabilities. 📌 Outcome Tribunal upheld inclusion of royalties/licence fees in customs valuation. Differential duty demands sustained. Confiscation and penalties confirmed (with modifications). Revenue’s appeals partly allowed. 📌 Impact Importers: Cannot escape liability by routing imports through contract manufacturers if they are the true beneficiaries. Customs Law: Reinforces substance over form—beneficial ownership determines liability. Policy: Signals stricter scrutiny of MNC structures using subsidiaries to avoid duty on royalties/licence fees. ✅ Takeaway: The Xiaomi ruling shows that in customs valuation, beneficial ownership matters more than legal form. If an entity controls imports, pays royalties, and reaps the commercial benefit, it will be treated as the beneficial owner and held liable for duty. #CESTAT #CustomsLaw #Xiaomi #BeneficialOwnership #Royalty #Valuation #IndirectTax #LegalUpdate

    Re-export of Imported Goods – Duty Drawback under Section 74, Customs Act, 1962 Many importers overlook that customs duty is not intended to be a cost where imported goods are subsequently re-exported. 📌 Statutory Framework Section 74 of the Customs Act, 1962 permits refund of up to 98% of customs duty paid on imported goods that are re-exported from India, subject to prescribed conditions. 🔍 Applicability This provision is particularly relevant for: • Capital goods and machinery • Project imports • Exhibition / demo goods • Rejected or surplus consignments 💰 Key Benefit • Re-export without use → Refund up to 98% of duty • Re-export after use → Refund allowed after depreciation, as per notified slabs ⚖️ Compliance Essentials • Goods must be identifiable as the same goods imported • No substantial transformation permitted • Shipping Bill to be filed specifically under Section 74 • Proper linkage with original Bill of Entry Important Clarification: There is no flat denial merely due to time lapse—refund depends on compliance and depreciation norms, not arbitrary timelines. 💡 Practical Insight For businesses dealing with temporary imports or capital equipment, Section 74 can significantly reduce landed cost and improve cash flow—provided documentation and procedures are handled correctly. 📚 Reference: Section 74, Customs Act, 1962 read with Customs & Central Excise Duties Drawback Rules. #CustomsLaw #Section74 #DutyDrawback #ReExport #EXIM #TradeCompliance #IndirectTax #IndianCustoms

The application is general; if a country does not have a free trade agreement covering the affected products, the TIGIE rates will apply. - While the measure seeks to counter unfair foreign competition and encourage local production, it may also increase costs for Mexican manufacturers that rely on imported inputs and components, potentially affecting prices and supply chains. - The changes are expected to enter into force on January 1, 2026, following publication in the Official Gazette of the Federation (DOF). 20251209-V.pdf https://lnkd.in/e7Xz5VP2

    📌 Import of Goods – Registration under GST (Part 4 - Final)   IGST on imports is mandatory – GST registration is not. Here’s why: It is clear that Import of Goods is termed as Inter-State supply and thus IGST is levied, and the importer is liable to pay IGST on value of Import. 🔍 The interesting point in this scenario is that IGST is not paid under FCM* nor under RCM** but it is collected in accordance with the provisions of The Customs Tariff Act, 1975 and not as per IGST Act, 2017. (Though it is levied as per IGST Act, 2017) And due to the above reason, an importer is not liable to get registered under GST for the payment of IGST on imports. 📌 However, it is mandatory for an importer (Registered under GST) to quote his/her GSTIN in the bill of entry for the purpose of payment of IGST on import of goods AND to avail Input Tax Credit (ITC) of such IGST.   ✅ This brings the import of goods series to a close. Hope it simplified a complex area. Happy to learn from your views or experiences. *FCM- Forward Charge Mechanism: When the Seller deposits the amount of GST to Government by himself. (For instance – When a seller (Registered under GST) sells a product to his customer and he himself deposits the amount of GST to Government) **RCM- Reverse Charge Mechanism: When the Recipient is liable to deposit the amount of GST to Government by himself. (For instance – Lawyer gives services to a person (registered under GST) then such person is liable to deposit GST to Government.)

    🔔 Upcoming VAT Law Amendments: Key Changes Effective 18 December 2025 The latest amendments to the Macedonian VAT Law were published in the Official Gazette of the Republic of North Macedonia on 10 December 2025 and will come into force on 18 December 2025. Based on these amendments, the import VAT exemption for low‑value shipments has been narrowed. The exemption now applies only to goods sent free of charge by one individual to another individual in Macedonia, where: (i) the goods are imported for non‑commercial purposes, and (ii) the value does not exceed EUR 22. Where a shipment includes two or more items and the total value exceeds EUR 22 (in denar equivalent), the exemption may be applied only to those items that would qualify if imported separately. A single item valued above EUR 22 cannot obtain a partial import VAT exemption by splitting its value. The exemption does not apply to imports of tobacco and tobacco products, alcohol and alcoholic beverages, perfumes, and toilet waters. Additionally, with these amendments, the previous broader import VAT exemption for shipments under EUR 22 delivered from abroad to recipients in North Macedonia has been repealed. Considering the forthcoming holiday season, these amendments are relevant for individuals and companies receiving gifts from abroad and for deliveries of purchases from foreign online e-commerce platforms into North Macedonia.
