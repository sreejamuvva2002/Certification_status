Your Hyundais And Kias Won't Explode In Price Thanks To Last-Minute Tariff Deal

By  Viknesh Vijayenthiran
Published Jul 31, 2025, 7:24 AM EDT

South Korea and the United States brokered a last-minute tariffs deal on Wednesday, meaning the price of cars from your favorite Korean brands like Hyundai and Kia won't explode higher, though there will likely still be some increases. Much like the deal signed with Europe and another with Japan earlier in July, the latest agreement lowers the auto tariff on cars and parts built in South Korea to 15%, down from the 25% that would be in effect after the August 1 “Liberation Day” deadline – when reciprocal tariffs were scheduled to be introduced.

The rate is one of the lowest under President Donald Trump's new tariff regime, despite South Korea having one of the biggest trade surpluses with the US among trading partners. According to Reuters, the surplus last year was a record $55.7 billion, up 25.4% year-on-year. Prior to the deal, South Korea and the US had a free trade agreement in place, and many US products being exported to South Korea will remain without tariffs under the new deal, including cars and agricultural products.

Deal Will Help American Automakers Too

In addition to the duty-free access for US exports to South Korea, the deal is also a boon for American automakers that import products from South Korea, like General Motors, whose Chevrolet Trax and Buick Envista are built in the country. Polestar is also geared to start building its Polestar 4 in South Korea later this year for export to the US. The Polestar 4 was originally due to be imported from China, but Polestar made arrangements to build the electric compact crossover in South Korea after the previous Biden administration introduced tariffs on Chinese-made EVs.

South Korea is also a major exporter of critical car parts used by manufacturers in the US, especially in the areas of computer chips, digital equipment, batteries for electric vehicles, and steel.

Buick Is On A Hot Streak, But Prolonged Tariffs Might End That

Sales are up, but nearly every Buick model is imported from China or South Korea.

By  Justin Westbrook

As part of the deal, South Korea agreed to invest $350 billion in the US in projects to be selected by the Trump administration, with the administration already confirming the bulk of the investment will go toward a shipbuilding partnership. South Korea also agreed to purchase $100 billion in energy products from the US. Full details of the deal haven't been announced, and it isn't clear how much of the investments and energy purchases were already in place prior to the deal.

Automakers Still Face Major Headwinds

Even with the tariff deal in place, Hyundai and Kia could still face as much as $5 billion in additional costs this year, according to Bloomberg. That financial hit could translate into higher sticker prices for US consumers, at a time when demand is slowing in South Korea and competition from Chinese firms is intensifying.

To offset the impact of tariffs, Korean automakers are expected to ramp up US production and redirect more exports to other markets. Hyundai, for instance, announced in March a record $21 billion investment plan in the US through 2028 – more than its total US investment since launching operations in 1986. That expansion includes boosting EV and battery manufacturing in Alabama and Georgia.

Tariffs Be Damned, Kia Is Building The EV4 In Korea

The affordable electric sedan will be subject to tariffs.

By  Ian Wright

Meanwhile, US automakers remain in a difficult position. They import roughly half of the vehicles they sell domestically, along with a large share of parts, much of it from countries that haven’t secured tariff deals, including Canada, China, and Mexico. With no exemptions in place, those imports face steep duties starting 1 August, putting further pressure on prices, supply chains, and margins.

Sources: Reuters, Bloomberg
