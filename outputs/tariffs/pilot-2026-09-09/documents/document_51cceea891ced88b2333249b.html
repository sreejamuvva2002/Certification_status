July 24 2026 - U.S. Imposes Section 301 Forced Labor Tariffs on Imports from Multiple Countries Starting July 24  

The United States Trade Representative (USTR) has unveiled a sweeping set of tariff actions following the conclusion of 60 investigations under Sections 301(b) and 304(a) of the Trade Act of 1974. The investigations found that certain acts, policies, and practices of the targeted economies are actionable under U.S. trade law, prompting decisive U.S. action as directed by the President. 

 

Tariffs Imposed Across Multiple Economies 

Under the new measures, the U.S. will impose tariffs on all products from the investigated economies, with specific exemptions detailed in official annexes. The actions are part of a coordinated effort to address unfair trade practices and, in some cases, to counteract the importation of goods linked to forced labor. 

 

Implementation Timeline: 

  • The new tariffs will take effect starting July 24, 2026, at 12:01 a.m. ET. Goods loaded before this date and arriving before July 28, 12:01 a.m. ET, will not be subject to the new duties. 

 

Country-Specific Tariff Application: 

  • 10% Tariff: 
    Applies to most goods imported from Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Canada, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, India, Indonesia, Jordan, Malaysia, Mexico, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, the United Kingdom, and Trinidad and Tobago. 
  • EU and Taiwan: 
    A combination of MFN and Section 301 duties will be applied to reach a flat 10% tariff rate. No additional Section 301 duty will be levied if the MFN rate exceeds 10%. 
  • Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland: 
    A combination of MFN and Section 301 duties will be applied to reach a flat 12.5% tariff. If the MFN rate is above 12.5%, no extra duty will be imposed. 
  • 12.5% Tariff Rate: 
    For all other affected economies, a 12.5% Section 301 tariff will apply. 
  • Tariff Rate Quotas (TRQs): 
    The USTR will establish TRQs for Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Malaysia based on their imports of U.S. cotton and textile goods. This measure is intended to encourage these countries to source more inputs from the U.S. and reduce reliance on suppliers potentially using forced labor. 

 

Exemptions: 

 

  • Articles that (1) were loaded onto a vessel (air, rail, and truck not included) at the port of loading and in transit on the final mode of transit (mother vessel) prior to entry into the United States before 12:01 a.m. eastern time on July 24, 2026; and (2) are entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, before 12:01 a.m. eastern time on July 28, 2026. 
  • Specific HTS listed, including articles of civil aircraft and articles for use in pharmaceutical applications, listed in Annex I and II of the Notice. These exemptions apply to all countries.  
  • Additional exemptions are in place for Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Ecuador, El Salvador, the EU, Guatemala, Indonesia, Jordan, Malaysia, Switzerland, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom. 
  • Articles that are donations by persons subject to the jurisdiction of the United States, such as food, clothing and medicine, intended to be used to relieve human suffering. 
  • Articles that are informational materials, including but not limited to publications, films, posters, phonograph records, photographs, microfilms, microfiche, tapes, compact disks, CD ROMs, artworks and news wire feeds. 

 

Additional Call Outs: 

 

Chapter 98 

  • The additional duties imposed by headings 9903.05.20–9903.05.84 shall not apply to goods for which entry is properly claimed under a provision of chapter 98 of the tariff schedule pursuant to applicable regulations of U.S. Customs and Border Protection (“CBP”), and whenever CBP agrees that entry under such a provision is appropriate, except for goods entered under subheadings 9802.00.40, 9802.00.50 or 9802.00.60 or heading 9802.00.80.  For goods entered under subheadings 9802.00.40, 9802.00.50 and 9802.00.60, the additional duties apply to the value of repairs, alterations or processing performed, as described in the applicable subheading.  For goods entered under heading 9802.00.80, the additional duties apply to the value of the article assembled abroad, less the cost or value of such products of the United States, as described.  
     

Foreign Trade Zones 

  • Any product of Section 301 Forced Labor that is subject to the additional duty imposed by this action, and that is admitted into a U.S. foreign trade zone, except any product that is eligible for admission under “domestic status” as defined in 19 C.F.R. 146.43, only may be admitted as “privileged foreign status,” as defined in 19 C.F.R. 146.41, effective as of the date that the additional duty is imposed.

July 21 2026 - Trump Announces 50% Tariffs on Select Canadian Imports, Effective August 19 

President Donald J. Trump has announced that the United States will impose a 50% tariff on certain products imported from Canada, citing unfair trade practices related to Canada’s motor vehicle tariff policies. The new duties, authorized under Section 338 of U.S. law, will take effect at 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on August 19, 2026. 

 

Section 338 grants the President the authority to impose additional tariffs—up to 50 percent—on imports from countries that are found to discriminate against U.S. commerce or create an unequal burden on American trade. The measure is intended as a direct response to what the administration says is Canada’s discriminatory treatment of U.S. exports, particularly in the automotive sector. 

“These additional duties are necessary to counteract the disadvantage imposed on U.S. commerce by Canada’s motor vehicle tariff scheme,” President Trump said in an official proclamation. He added that the tariffs would remain in place unless modified or terminated by further executive action. 

 

The new tariffs will apply to a list of Canadian products detailed in an annex to the presidential proclamation. These duties are in addition to any existing tariffs, taxes, or fees that may already apply to the affected goods. Notably, products currently subject to separate duties under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 or those covered by the World Trade Organization Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft (excluding unmanned aircraft) will be exempt from the new measure. 

 

According to the proclamation, goods subject to the new duties and entered into U.S. foreign trade zones after the effective date must be admitted under “privileged foreign status,” ensuring they are subject to the full tariff upon entry for domestic consumption. 

The administration has also reserved the right to suspend, revoke, or amend the new tariffs as needed, should circumstances change or if the public interest so requires. 

 

Canadian officials have not yet commented on the new tariffs or indicated whether retaliatory measures are being considered. 

 

For a complete list of products affected, refer to Annex II of the official proclamation.

Mar 04 2026 - Court of International Trade Orders CBP to Liquidate All Entries Where IEEPA Duties Were Paid 

 

Today, March 4, 2026, the U.S. Court of International Trade (CIT) has ordered U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) to liquidate all unliquidated entries subject to IEEPA duties without applying those duties. Additionally, any entries that have been liquidated but are not yet final must be reliquidated without IEEPA duties. 

 

A customs entry is liquidated but not yet final if:

  • The timeframe for challenging the liquidation (such as filing a protest under 19 U.S.C. §1514) is still open, typically 180 days from the date of liquidation.
  • There is an ongoing legal action, protest, or court case regarding that entry.

 

The order comes in response to the recent Supreme Court decision, which found that the IEEPA does not grant authority for tariff imposition.

 

At this point, we are still waiting on additional information from U.S. Customs and Border Protection. We will continue to stay abreast of the situation and relay important updates as they become available.

Feb 25 2026 - Transition from IEEPA Duties to Section 122 Surcharge

 

As you may be aware, on February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose tariffs, thereby invalidating all IEEPA‑based tariff measures implemented over the past year. Other tariff authorities such as Section 301 and Section 232 remain in full effect.

 

In parallel, the Administration announced a temporary import surcharge under Section122 of the Trade Act of 1974, initially set at 10% and authorized up to 15%, effective February 24, 2026 for up to 150 days unless modified sooner.

 

CBP Updates: IEEPA Removal + New Section 122 Surcharge:

CBP has now issued two critical CSMS notices governing this transition:

 

1. CSMS #67834313 – Ending Collection of IEEPA Duties

IEEPA duties will not be collected on any goods entered or withdrawn for consumption on or after 12:00 a.m. ET, February 24, 2026.

 

ACE is actively being updated and IEEPA HTS lines have been removed / inactivated for rating and collection.

 

This change affects IEEPA only, and does not alter Section 232 or Section 301 obligations.


2. CSMS #67844987 – Imposing Temporary Section 122 Duties

CBP has released detailed implementation instructions for the new Section 122 surcharge:

 

Effective Period: 

Applies to articles of any country entered or withdrawn for consumption from 12:01 a.m. EST, Feb 24, 2026 through 12:01 a.m. EDT, July 24, 2026.

All products of any country are subject to an additional 10% ad valorem duty, except those qualifying for the specific exemptions listed in headings 9903.03.02 through 9903.03.11.

What happened: 
On Friday February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. The White House immediately issued an order ending all IEEPA based ad valorem duties “as soon as practicable.” 

 

What this covers: IEEPA tariffs that had applied to multiple programs—including the “reciprocal” tariffs and the IEEPA duties tied to specific countries or issues (e.g., Canada, Mexico, China opioid supply chain, Brazil, India/Russian oil, Cuba, Iran)—are to be terminated and cease collection once implementing instructions are issued. Feb 22 2026 - Trump Imposes 10% Section 122 Tariff with Multiple Exemptions 

On February 20, President Donald Trump issued a series of executive orders and proclamations establishing a 10% across-the-board tariff under Section 122, set to take effect at 12:01 a.m. on February 24. Subsequently, on Saturday, February 21, 2026, President Trump posted on social media that he is raising the 10% Worldwide Tariff on Countries up to the fully allowed and legally tested 15% level effective immediately. Currently, there has not been an executive order issued confirming this increase. 

 

What we know now is that the tariffs will be in effect for goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, on or after 12:01 a.m. eastern standard time on February 24, 2026, through 12:01 a.m. eastern daylight time on July 24, 2026. At the same time, the President directed that tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) be lifted "as soon as practicable," while maintaining the current suspension of de minimis exemptions. 

 

According to the proclamation, goods qualifying for the USMCA will be exempt from the new Section 122 tariff, as will all products currently subject to Section 232 tariffs or those that may become subject to such tariffs in the future. Exemptions granted under the reciprocal tariffs recently struck down by the Supreme Court will also be preserved under the Section 122 tariff, including certain agricultural products, pharmaceuticals, electronics, passenger vehicles and trucks, aerospace products, and informational materials. Detailed lists of these exemptions are included in an annex to the proclamation, reflecting those previously outlined in Annex II of the reciprocal tariffs. Additionally, textiles and apparel entering duty-free from CAFTA-DR countries will be exempt. 

 

The proclamation provides an “in transit” clause, exempting goods that are loaded and in transit before 12:01 a.m. on February 24 and entered into the U.S. before 12:01 a.m. on February 28. Goods subject to the new tariff will be classified under tariff subheading 9903.03.01, with subheadings 9903.03.02 through 9903.03.11 designated for various exemptions. 

 

Further, the proclamation requires that items admitted to U.S. foreign-trade zones and subject to Section 122 tariffs must be entered under privileged foreign status. The U.S. Trade Representative is tasked with notifying the president of any developments that could warrant a suspension, modification, or termination of the tariff, or necessitate further action under Section 122. If any specific exemptions are eliminated, the removal will only apply prospectively from the date of invalidation—tariffs will not be collected retroactively. 

 

Key Details: 

  • 10% tariff on all goods (except those with exemptions) from all countries.
  • In effect for goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, on or after 12:01 a.m. eastern standard time on February 24, 2026, through 12:01 a.m. eastern daylight time on July 24, 2026.
  • Exemptions:
    • Goods that qualify for USMCA

Bonds

 

  • Bond considerations with the additional tariffs and the duty amounts paid by importers drastically increasing, below are some notes to consider: 
  • Based on CBP regulations, the importer’s continuous bond must cover 10% of duties, taxes, fees, ADD/CVD and new tariffs (232s, 301s, 201s) over a rolling 12-month period.  
  • Sufficient bond value coverage reduces the risk of the accumulation of bond liability and bond stacking.  
  • Bond stacking is having more than one customs bond open within a 12-month period which creates cumulative liability and additional risk. 
  • If CBP deems a bond is saturated, the bond is terminated and requires a new higher bond value to be on file with CBP.  A 30-day deadline for compliance starts. 
  • A bond termination requires advance notice of 15 days, if a saturated bond is not timely replaced and is rendered insufficient by CBP, the importer will not be able to transmit new ISF and customs entries during this time period.  
  • Increased bond value amounts & bond stacking create risk for the surety.   

  • The surety’s underwriter in order to protect their interests and manage risk may require collateral from the importer by means of a letter of credit.

April 3 2025 - Revocation of De Minimis for China

 

President Donald J. Trump signed an Executive Order on April 2, 2025 eliminating duty-free de minimis treatment for low-value imports from China and Hong Kong, effective May 2, 2025. This move aims to counter the flow of synthetic opioids into the U.S.

 

Key provisions

 

  • All non-postal imports valued at $800 or less will now be subject to full applicable duties.
  • Postal imports valued at $800 or less will face a 30% duty or $25 per item (rising to $50 per item after June 1, 2025), replacing all other prior tariffs.
  • Carriers must report shipments, hold international carrier bonds, and remit duties to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP).
  • CBP can require formal entry for any postal package.
  • The Commerce Secretary will report within 90 days on the Order’s impact and potential expansion to Macau.

 

This policy tightens import controls while ensuring tariff revenue collection from low-value shipments previously exempt from duties.

April 3 2025 - Adjusting imports of automobiles and automobile parts into the United States – annexes released 

 

As follow up to the Presidential Proclamation issued on March 26, 2025, 90 FR 14705 was published in the Federal Register outlining the specifics of the order. 

 

Key takeaways

 

  • On February 17, 2019, the Secretary of Commerce reported that imports of automobiles and certain automobile parts threaten U.S. national security.
  • The report identified that these imports are being brought in such quantities and under such circumstances that they impair national security.
  • The U.S. automotive industry's challenges include material and parts shortages, labor shortages, and increased competition from foreign industries with unfair subsidies.
  • Agreements such as the United States-Korea Free Trade Agreement and the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) have not sufficiently mitigated the national security threat posed by these imports.
  • To address the ongoing threat, a 25% tariff on imported automobiles will be imposed starting April 3, 2025.
  • Tariffs on auto parts will begin on May 3,2025. 
  • For automobiles qualifying for preferential treatment under the USMCA, tariffs will apply only to the non-U.S. content of the vehicle, provided accurate documentation is submitted and verified.
  • No drawback (refund) of the imposed tariffs will be available.

 

 

Detailed information

On February 17, 2019, the Secretary of Commerce reported that imports of automobiles and certain automobile parts were threatening U.S. national security. Subsequent actions, including Proclamation 9888 of May 17, 2019, concurred with this finding and initiated negotiations to address the threat. However, these negotiations did not yield the desired agreements, and recent updates indicate that national security concerns have escalated, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and supply chain vulnerabilities. 

 

To address this ongoing threat, a 25% ad valorem tariff will be imposed on imported automobiles starting April 3, 2025. For automobile parts, the tariffs will be implemented by May 3, 2025. Importers of automobiles qualifying for preferential treatment under the USMCA may submit documentation detailing U.S. content, with the tariff applied only to the non-U.S. content. Inaccurate declarations will result in the full value of the automobile being subject to the tariff, both retroactively and prospectively. The 25% tariff will not apply to automobile parts under the USMCA until a process is established to apply the tariff exclusively to non-U.S. content.

April 3 2025 - Bond Sufficiency Reminder 

 

Key takeaways

 

The continuous import bond amount calculation is based on 10% of the total duties, taxes and fees paid or payable in a 12 month period.  

 

Please note that the bond coverage is a rolling continuous 12 months. Therefore, to ensure proper bond sufficiency coverage, estimated future duties, taxes, sanctions, tariffs, fees, ADD/CVD, etc. need to be calculated on the value of anticipated import volumes. 

 

If GEODIS is your bond management provider, bond sufficiency reports can be requested in order to review your current bond saturation.  Furthermore, GEODIS can assist in determining the proper bond sufficiency going forward associated with the impact of the new tariffs on your total bond amount.  If GEODIS does not manage your customs bond, please contact GEODIS Trade Services to discuss bond coverage solutions.   

 

CBP is tasked with monitoring the bond amounts and if saturation occurs only 15 days are allotted to comply before the bond is deactivated. 

 

Therefore, we recommend that proactive measures be taken to analyze and access accordingly.
