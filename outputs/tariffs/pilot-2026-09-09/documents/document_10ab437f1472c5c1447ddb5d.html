Hey everyone, Misco Electric here. Today is Sunday February 15th, 2026 and this is the current your weekly e-news in about 10 minutes. The US Department of Commerce announced final affirmative determinations in its anti dumping and countervailing duty investigations of active battery anode material imported from China this week, with details published in the Federal Register expected next week. Primarily graphite based active anode material is a critical component in lithium ion battery anodes used in electric vehicle batteries, energy storage systems and other applications. In this determination, the investigators. Claim that Chinese producers are selling active anode material in the US at less than fair value and receiving counter available government subsidies. Key final rates including anti dumping duties of 93.5% for many specific exporters and producers including companies tied to Tesla and Panasonic and a China wide rate of 102.72% for others. Countervailing duties were recorded at approximately 66.8. Percent across most producers and exporters combined. These could result in total duties exceeding 160% on many imports of Chinese active anode materials. To note, the determinations exclude anode active materials already processed into finished lithium batteries, EV packs, or related end products. This decision shelters US domestic producers of anode active materials who petitioned for relief in late 2024, arguing that heavily subsidized. Been dumped Chinese imports have hindered their profit margins and industry development for the US EV battery supply chain. The tariffs aimed to reduce reliance on Chinese dominated graphite anode materials, encourage domestic or allied sourcing, and potentially raise costs for battery manufacturers using imported active anode materials, though some offsets apply for subsidies in cash deposit calculations, China provides over 80% of graphite anode materials to the globe. Pure graphite anodes account for the majority, often cited at nearly 80% of the Evie anode market. The remaining share includes graphite, silicon composites, and a small fraction of more advanced options like silicon rich or emerging alternatives. The duties are subject to final affirmative injury confirmation from the US International Trade Commission, after which US Customs and Border Protection will begin collecting them, as with any competition, limiting market distortion. Will likely near term result will be a higher overall cost of domestic battery production. Speaking of the consequences of protectionism, Chinese electric vehicle giant VYD filed a lawsuit against the US government. They've challenged the legality of tariffs imposed under President Donald Trump's administration. The complaint, lodged in January by 4 of BYOD US subsidiaries in the US Court of International Trade, targets duties enacted since April of 2025 using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act BYOD argues that the law. Does not authorize the President to impose tariffs or equivalent border taxes, as the statute tax lacks such terminology or equivalent meaning. The company is seeking to invalidate 9 related executive orders, halt their enforcement, and obtain refunds, including interest, for all tariffs it has paid on imported materials, components, and products supporting its American operations, which include electric buses, trucks, batteries, and energy storage systems. This marks the first known legal challenge by a Chinese automaker against these US tariffs, though thousands of other global firms have filed similar suits. The case unfolds as a Supreme Court decision on the broader validity of Trump's tariff authority remains pending. BYOD maintains its filed independently to safeguard its refund rights amid uncertainty. A successful outcome could lower costs for BYOD US linked activities and potentially reshape access for Chinese EV. Auto show involved Farley meeting with figures such as U.S. trade Representative Jameson Greer, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and EPA administrator Lee Zeldin. The proposed structure would require US partners to hold a controlling stake in any joint venture, with profits and technology shared between the parties. The approach aims to provide some safeguards for domestic automakers while enabling Chinese firms to build cars on American soil. There are many moving pieces.     Chinese EVs look shockingly affordable in China. Models comparable to a VW ID.4 (starting from 42.635 € in Germany) can be twice cheaper. But they won't be as cheap in the EU, and here's why. 1. Massive domestic state support. Chinese EV makers receive substantial government support: direct grants, tax relief, preferential loans, and local incentives. This is already reflected in the EU import policies. A way to circumvent tariff restrictions is to produce cars in the EU or in a jurisdiction with a zero trade tariff with the EU. And there is no way to avoid VAT (average rate 21.8% in 2026). 2. Homologation and regulatory compliance costs. GDPR, EU Data Act, firmware localization, distribution and service networks, warranty repairs, and after-sales obligations. All these costs erase a large part of the price advantage before a Chinese EV even reaches a showroom. 3. Price wars. China’s EV market is hyper‑competitive, but also oversupplied. Hundreds of thousands of electric vehicles are waiting for months in giant parking lots, unable to find buyers. OEMs accept lower margins to defend market share while relying on scale, subsidies, and high degree of vertical integration (access to raw materials, in-house R&D and production of batteries, power electronics and other components) to stay profitable. Once these cars enter the EU market, these advantages largely disappears. Chinese EVs are cheap in China because of a unique mix of state support, brutal domestic competition, and vertically integrated industrial policy. Selling at Chinese price levels in Europe would simply mean financial losses. I'm following the activities of the Chinese brand Zeekr from Geely's portfolio; I like their design and specs are cool. Some time ago they officially entered the German market, the portfolio consists of four models. Zeekr 7 GT starts from EUR 48k. In China the same model (sold by name 007 GT) starts from EUR 23k. Bottom line: Chinese brands can still be competitive in Europe. Some of them are expanding their presence, buying European brands, building factories and developing distribution networks. But expecting China prices in the EU is unrealistic. #EV #ElectricVehicles #AutomotiveIndustry #China #Europe #EVMarket

    The global EV revolution is happening, except in the U.S. and Canada, largely influenced by government policies. Chinese EVs are outselling ICEVs in the country. EV sales in the EU reached 34% market share in 2025, 18% of which were Chinese imports subject to varying EU import tariffs plus duties, ranging from 18% to 45%. EU emission standards remain intact and scheduled to get more stringent after 2027. Less than 2 years ago, the EU future for EVs looked dim. In 2024, the Volkswagen Group had regressed on EVs, indicating that the EV transition was costing too much.  Plus, VW EV sales were disappointing in both Europe and China, the latter representing 40% of the VW global market. VW went through a metamorphosis in 2025, going full tilt on EVs in both Europe and China.  VW expanded its lineup of attractively priced EVs in Europe and, in a timeframe of 18 months, from concept to production, created a unique China Electronic Architecture to introduce 10 China-specific new models EU manufacturers were compelled to offer competitive affordable EV lineups. With mid-level tariff in the EU, Indonesia and elsewhere, Chinese EV and battery brands are pursuing manufacturing projects in these jurisdictions to circumvent tariffs. In contrast, Canada and the U.S. EV sales are declining or plateauing in a drastically changing political landscape such that Trump eliminated U.S. vehicle emission standards; Canada abandoned EV regulated market share targets; and 100% tariffs on Chinese EV imports remain intact in both countries. Canada’s new EV strategy is to achieve 90% EV sales by 2040, which compares poorly to the EU 90% EV market share target by 2035. Ford, Honda, GM and other North American manufactures are curtailing or abandoning EV plans and projects, with greater emphasis on PHEVs and the more profitable gas-powered SUVs and pickups. China will continue to be the frontrunner. The widespread belief that Chinese EVs are highly subsidized and use cheap labour, thus are lower priced, is hard to validate. The Chinese government heavily invests in innovation which reduces manufacturers' cost recovery time, while both the U.S. and Canada offer tax credits and other forms of subsidies for new manufacturing plants. Nearly all, 95%, of Chinese EVs are equipped with the less costly lithium-ion phosphate batteries and the even more inexpensive sodium-ion batteries will enter China’s mass market in 2027. Mind boggling, there are over 20 million chargers in China, exceeding the numbers of fuel pumps. Now the big Chinese EV story is electric medium- and heavy-duty trucks which have already captured over half of the domestic market. Several Chinese electric truck manufacturers have begun exporting their products. U.S. and Canadian automakers cannot indefinitely protect themselves from global EV progress.
