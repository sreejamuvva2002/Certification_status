    Breaking Down the US-China Trade Tariffs: What’s in Effect Now?

    July 24, 2026 Posted by China Briefing Written by Arendse Huld
    Available language
    In this article

      US-China tariff rates have become increasingly complex, shaped by years of escalating trade actions and policy shifts. With overlapping measures such as Section 301 tariffs, the temporary global tariff, and product-specific controls, understanding what applies in 2025 can be challenging. This article clarifies which US-China tariff rates are currently in effect and how and when they overlap.

      UPDATE: (23 July 2026): The United States Trade Representative (USTR) has announced a new 12.5 percent Section 301 tariff on Chinese goods for the country’s “failure to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor”, effective 24 July 2026. In practice, this action raises the overall tariff on Chinese goods by 2.5 percent.

      Certain goods are exempted from the tariffs, including all goods already subject to Section 232 tariffs, such as those on steel and aluminium.

      The tariffs are a permanent replacement for the tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) that the US Supreme Court struck down in February. The stop-gap 10 percent tariff measure imposed following the Supreme Court ruling was set to expire on 24 July 2026.

      A second Section 301 tariff investigation into excess capacity in 60 countries, launched in March 2026, is still ongoing. It remains unclear whether any action will be taken under this investigation.

      China launched retaliatory investigations into US trade barriers after the US announced the two Section 301 investigations in March. However, no subsequent action has been taken at this time. China has previously indicated that it would accept the replacement tariffs, provided they do not exceed the rate set in the agreement reached in November 2025 – a combined total of 20 percent. With a 12.5 percent duty applied under the forced labour schedule, that indicates the US should set no more than an 7.5 percent tariff under the excess capacity investigation, or risk crossing China’s red line.

      UPDATE: (2 June 2026): The US Trade Representative (USTR) has proposed a new tariff of 12.5 percent on China as a result of a Section 301 investigation into forced labour. The investigation, one of two launched on 12 March, seeks to reinstate tariffs on trading partners following the Supreme Court’s decision striking down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), a core tenet of Trump’s tariff regime. The USTR alleges that China, along with 59 other countries under investigation, has “failed both to impose a forced labour import prohibition and to effectively enforce such a prohibition”.

      The USTR is collecting comments on the proposal until 6 July, before a hearing on 7 July. A final decision will be issued after this date.

      While China has condemned the move, it stopped short of announcing any countermeasures. This may be due to an agreement reached between the two sides during Trump’s recent visit to Beijing that the US could reinstate tariffs, provided they do not surpass the rate negotiated as part of the Busan agreement. However, another Section 301 investigation into excess capacity is still ongoing, and any additional tariffs proposed as a result could exceed this threshold.

      UPDATE (20 May 2026): China and the US appear to have agreed on terms for the restoration of the US’s now-voided tariffs on China, but have not yet agreed on an extension to the one-year trade truce negotiated in October last year.

      In an interview with Reuters on 19 May, which came after the long-awaited Xi-Trump meetings in Beijing the week prior, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said he believed that China would accept the restoration of the prior tariff rates through the Section 301 investigations, “as long as they don’t go higher”. The two investigations are set to be concluded this summer.

      The Chinese side has obliquely confirmed this. In a 20 May press briefing, the Chinese Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) told reporters that it hoped the US would “honour its commitments” and ensure that “the level of US tariffs on China will not exceed the level” agreed upon following the Trump-Xi meeting in Busan in October. This meeting resulted in the two sides agreeing to cut two-way tariffs and suspend a range of other trade barriers.

      This suggests that the two Section 301 tariff investigations, initiated in March following the Supreme Court’s decision to remove the two tariff lines imposed on China under IEEPA, will each result in a 10 percent duty on Chinese goods, replacing the 10 percent “fentanyl” and 10 percent reciprocal tariff imposed under IEEPA.

      It remains unclear whether this means China will rescind the investigations launched in retaliation for the two Section 301 investigations.

      While this is not a final resolution to the trade dispute, agreeing on the terms of the tariff restorations reduces the risk that the Section 301 investigations instigate another tariff war.

      When asked whether the one-year truce negotiated in Busan would be extended, Bessent said that the US was “not in a rush to extend it”, adding that “Things are stable”.

      The US-China trade relationship is shaped by a confusing web of interweaving import duties that have been levied, adjusted, revoked, and reinstated going back to 2018. This complex tariff regime, which was launched under the first Trump administration and continued throughout the Biden presidency, has become ever more volatile since Trump retook office in January 2025.

      The complexity arises from multiple overlapping tariffs, which span duties implemented under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 (the “Trade Act”) that seek to tackle China’s purported unfair trade practices, duties imposed on certain products under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 related to national security concerns, and a 15 percent global tariff in effect from 24 February 2026, announced after the US Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). Previous fentanyl-related tariffs addressing the illicit drug trade and reciprocal tariffs addressing the US’s trade deficit imposed under the IEEPA have since been eliminated by the Supreme Court decision.

      Due to the multitude of existing duties, the effective tariff rate on many Chinese goods shipped to the US remains close to 30 percent – still the highest of any country.

      This article breaks down both the history and the latest developments of US tariffs on China, and outlines what the tariff rates are today. 

      US tariffs on China 

      US tariffs on China date back to 2018, when Trump launched one of the most extensive tariff regimes in modern trade history. Many of these tariffs that have been implemented over the years are stacked, meaning multiple duties apply simultaneously on the same goods, significantly increasing the overall cost of imports from China. 

      According to Global Trade Alert, the trade-weighted average tariff rate on China dropped from 36.8 percent to 29.7 percent when the 15 percent global tariff was implemented following the Supreme Court decision.

      Section 232 tariffs 

       

      • May 2018: US imposes 10% tariff on aluminium imports and 25% tariff on steel imports on all countries.
      • 12 March 2025: US raises tariff on aluminium from 10% to 25% for all countries, removes country exemptions to reinstate 25% steel tariff on all countries.
      • 3 April 2025: US imposes 25% tariff on automobiles and automobile parts on all countries.
      • 3 June 2025: US raises tariff on steel and aluminium, and derivatives, to 50%, effective June 4.
      • 16 June 2025: US imposes 50% tariff on certain household appliances containing steel and aluminium, effective June 23.
      • 30 July 2025: US imposes 50% tariff on a range of semi-finished copper products and copper-intensive derivative goods, effective 1 August 2025.
      • 29 September 2025: US imposes 10% tariff on softwood time and 25% tariff on kitchen cabinets and upholstered wooden furniture, effective 14 October.

      While the Section 301 tariffs garnered the most attention during Trump’s first term in office, the Section 232 tariffs predate the trade war. These tariffs are imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, which authorises the US president to impose trade restrictions on goods that “threaten to impair” US national security.

      On 18 March 2018, the Trump administration imposed a 10 percent tariff on all aluminium imports and a 25 percent tariff on all steel imports to the US. The administration later negotiated exemptions for a range of countries, but the tariffs on Chinese steel and aluminium have remained in place ever since. 

      On 19 February 2025, Trump signed a proclamation announcing a 25 percent ad valorem tariff on all steel shipments arriving in the US and raising tariffs on aluminium imports from 10 to 25 percent. The tariffs are applicable to imports from all countries and regions “without exception”, and took effect on 12 March 2025. 

      As the prior steel and aluminium tariffs on China were never lifted, the effective tariff rate on Chinese steel products remained unchanged, while the tariff on aluminium products rose by 15 percentage points. Moreover, many steel and aluminium products coming from China have been separately subject to a 25 percent duty under the Section 301 action. 

      On 26 March 2025, Trump announced a 25 percent tariff on imports of automobiles and automobile parts from all trade partners, effective 3 April 2025. Many vehicles and vehicle parts coming from China were already subject to tariffs of up to 25 percent under Section 301.

      On 31 May 2025, Trump announced he would raise the tariff rate on steel and aluminium imports from all countries to 50 percent from 4 June.

      The Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), a department under the Department of Commerce, published a notice on 16 June 2025 extending the 50 percent steel and aluminium tariff to several household appliances containing steel, effective 23 June  2025. The new tariff, which will be levied on the proportion of steel contained in the product rather than the entire item, will be levied on the following steel derivative items:

      1. Combined refrigerator-freezers (HTSUS: 8418.10.00)
      2. Small and large dryers (HTSUS: 8451.21.00 and 8451.29.00)
      3. Washing machines (HTSUS: 8450.11.00 and 8450.20.00)
      4. Dishwashers (HTSUS: 8422.11.00)
      5. Chest and upright freezers (HTSUS: 8418.30.00 and 8418.40.00)
      6. Cooking stoves, ranges, and ovens (HTSUS: 8516.60.40)
      7. Food waste disposals (HTSUS: 8509.80.20)
      8. Welded wire rack (HTSUS: 9403.99.9020)

      While the above products will be taxed on their steel content, welded wire rack products will also be taxed on their aluminium content.

      On 39 July 2025, President Donald Trump signed a presidential proclamation under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, imposing a 50 percent tariff on a range of semi-finished copper products and copper-intensive derivative goods, effective 1 August 2025.

      • Affected imports include copper pipes, wires, rods, sheets, tubes, fittings, cables, connectors, and electrical components.
      • Exempted items include refined copper, ores, concentrates, cathodes, and scrap.

      According to the White House fact sheet:

      • The tariffs apply only to the copper content of a product; the non-copper portion remains subject to existing duties.
      • The copper tariffs do not stack with other Section 232 tariffs, such as those on automobiles—whichever applies first will prevail.
      • A product inclusion process will be created to allow for additional copper derivatives to be added to the tariff list.

      The Section 301 tariffs, first imposed in 2018 during Trump’s first term, were, until recently, the most sweeping China-specific tariffs, ultimately affecting goods of US$550 billion in value and setting off the first US-China trade war. 

      The initial Section 301 tariffs came into effect on 6 July 2018, when the US began imposing a 25 percent tariff on 818 imported Chinese products included in List 1, valued at US$34 billion. The affected products include various goods under the headings of inorganic and organic chemicals, pharmaceutical products, rubber, iron, steel, aluminium, nuclear reactors, electrical machinery and equipment, vehicles, aircraft, and many others.

      The second round of Section 301 tariffs – List 2 – came into force on 23 August 2018, with a 25 percent tariff on 279 goods originating from China worth a total of US$16 billion. Goods targeted include semiconductors, chemicals, plastics, motorbikes, and electric scooters, among many others. 

      The third round – List 3 – came into effect on 24 September 2018, with an initial rate of 10 percent on US$200 billion worth of Chinese goods. This was later increased to 25 percent, effective 10 May 2019, after trade talks stalled. 

      On 1 September 2019, the US began implementing an initial tariff of 15 percent on more than US$125 billion worth of Chinese imports under List 4A, affecting a range of products from footwear, diapers, and food products to smart watches, dishwashers, and flat-panel televisions. 

      List 4 was initially set to cover US$300 billion worth of Chinese goods. However, tariffs on these remaining goods, originally set to take effect on 15 December 2019, were suspended as part of the terms of Phase One of the US-China trade deal. Additionally, the 15 percent rate levied on goods under List 4A was halved to 7.5 percent, effective 14 February 2020.  

      Tariff increases following four-year review 

      On 13 September 2024, the Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) announced final modifications to the tariff actions under the Section 301 investigation, following a statutory four-year review. The modifications see tariffs ranging from 25 to 100 percent across 14 product groups, primarily in sectors targeted by China for dominance or where the US has recently made significant domestic investments. 

      Effective 27 September 2024, tariff rates on the following goods increased:

      • Battery parts (non-lithium-ion batteries) – Increased to 25 percent
      • Electric vehicles – Increased to 100 percent
      • Facemasks – Increased to no less than 25 percent
      • Lithium-ion electric vehicle batteries – Increased to 25 percent
      • Other critical minerals – Increased to 25 percent
      • Ship-to-shore cranes – Increased to 25 percent
      • Solar cells (whether or not assembled into modules) – Increased to 50 percent
      • Steel and aluminium products – Increased to 25 percent
      • Syringes and needles – Increased to no less than 50 percent 

      Meanwhile, the following tariffs came into effect on 1 January 2025: 

      • Semiconductors – Increased to 50 percent

      Finally, the following tariffs will come into effect on 1 January 2026: 

      • Lithium-ion non-electrical vehicle batteries – Increased to 25 percent
      • Medical gloves – Increased to no less than 25 percent
      • Natural graphite – Increased to 25 percent
      • Permanent magnets – Increased to 25 percent 

      Forced labour tariffs

      On 23 July 2026, the USTR officially imposed a 12.5 percent tariff on all Chinese goods, due to the country’s “failure to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor”. The new duty took effect on 24 July 2026.

      The tariffs were initially proposed on 2 June, and are designed as a permanent replacement for the tariffs imposed under IEEPA that the US Supreme Court struck down in February. The stop-gap 10 percent tariff measure imposed following the Supreme Court ruling was set to expire on 24 July.

      A second Section 301 tariff investigation into excess capacity in 60 countries, launched in March, is still ongoing. It remains unclear whether any action will be taken under this investigation.

      General Section 301 tariff exemptions

      For the initial Section 301 tariffs and the follow-up four-year review tariffs, both the Trump and Biden administrations have issued tariff exclusions for certain products over the years, including broad exemptions during the COVID-19 pandemic for goods deemed essential by the USTR. 

      Many of those exclusions have since expired. However, the USTR has continued to extend Section 301 tariff exclusions for 164 Chinese products. Following its four-year review, the USTR also granted 14 new exclusions for solar manufacturing equipment, effective retroactively from 1 January 2024. The exclusions on these 178 products were initially set to expire on 31 May 2025, but have since been extended until 10 November 2026, following the meeting between Xi Jinping and Trump.

      On 15 October 2024, the USTR launched an electronic portal for requesting tariff exclusions for machinery under HTS headings 84 and 85, covering items like manufacturing equipment and electrical machinery. Companies and trade groups can apply if they demonstrate that the equipment is essential and not reasonably available from domestic sources.

      Forced labour tariff exemptions

      Unlike the general Section 301 tariffs, the 12.5 percent forced labour tariff will not be stacked on goods also subject to the Section 232 tariffs. While the USTR announcement does not specify whether this duty stacks with the other Section 301 tariffs, it can be presumed that it does, as it is designed to replace the IEEPA tariffs, which were applied on top of the original Section 301 tariffs.

      In addition, a range of other goods have been exempted, including:

      • Raw materials that could become unavailable domestically if subject to these tariffs
      • Products that could cause economy-wide disruptions if subject to these tariffs
      • Products that cannot be grown or produced in sufficient quantities in the US or obtained from other sources
      • Products for which these tariffs may not be effective in obtaining the elimination of the acts, policies, and practices of economies found to be actionable in the investigations

      The full scope of exemptions is detailed in Annex I and Annex II of the Federal Register notice.

      IEEPA tariffs

       

      • 4 February 2025: 10 percent “fentanyl” tariff on all Chinese goods takes effect.
      • 4 March 2025: Tariff rate raised to 20 percent.
      • 2 April 2025: Trump announces a 34% reciprocal tariff on China.
      • 9 April 2025: 34% reciprocal tariff on China takes effect.
      • 8-9 April 2025: Trump raises reciprocal tariff on China to 84%, then 125%, effective immediately, following retaliatory action from China.
      • 14 May 2025: Reciprocal tariff on China reduced to 10% for 90 days following agreement. 
      • 12 August 2025: 10% reciprocal tariff rate extended for another 90 days following agreement. 
      • 30 October 2025: Xi-Trump meeting: Fentanyl tariff rate reduced to 10%, reduced reciprocal tariff rate of 10% extended for one year.
      • 14 November 2025: US exempts some agricultural goods not grown in the US from reciprocal tariff.
      • 20 February 2026: Supreme Court strikes down IEEPA tariffs.

       

      On 1 February 2025, Trump signed an executive order imposing an additional 10 percent tariff on all Chinese goods entering the country, ostensibly “to take the actions necessary to stem the flow of precursor chemicals to known criminal cartels and shut down money laundering by transnational criminal organisations”, per a White House Fact Sheet. 

      On 3 March 2025, through another executive order, Trump raised this tariff rate to 20 percent, with the stated reason that China “has not taken adequate steps to alleviate the illicit drug crisis through cooperative enforcement actions”. The 20 percent tariff rate took effect on 4 March 2025.

      On 2 April 2025, Trump announced his so-called “Liberation Day” tariffs, also known as the “reciprocal tariffs”, on around 90 trade partners. On Chinese products, he imposed a 34 percent duty to be levied on top of the 20 percent fentanyl tariffs.

      Following retaliatory tariffs by China on American goods, Trump signed an executive order raising the reciprocal tariff from 34 percent to 84 percent, and a day later announced the rate would rise further to 125 percent, bringing the final tariff rate on China to 145 percent, effective immediately.  

      On 12 May 2025, following a meeting between Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng, the US Secretary of the Treasury Scott Bessent, and US Trade Representative Ambassador Jamieson Greer in Geneva, China and the US agreed to lower reciprocal tariff rates from 125 percent to just 10 percent for a period of 90 days.

      Under the agreement, both sides committed to entirely cancel the higher reciprocal tariff rates imposed in succession from 9 April while the 34 percent rate initially imposed by the US on 2 April was amended to 10 percent for an initial period of 90 days.

      On 11 August 2025, President Trump signed an executive order extending the current tariff truce with China by another 90 days, moving the expiration date to 10 November 2025.

      On 30 October 2025, following a meeting between Trump and Xi in Busan, South Korea, the two sides agreed to extend the lowered 10 percent tariff rate until 10 November 2025. They also agreed to extend certain tariff exemptions. The US additionally cancelled the additional 10 percent fentanyl tariff announced in March, bringing the final rate down to 10 percent, in return for the lifting of the retaliatory tariffs levied on US agricultural goods.

      On 20 February, in a six-to-three decision, the Supreme Court found that Trump had exceeded his authority in implementing sweeping tariffs under the IEEPA, clarifying that the power to impose import tariffs under the US Constitution lies exclusively with Congress.

      The ruling means that the 10 percent “fentanyl” tariff and 10 percent reciprocal tariff levied on Chinese goods are no longer in effect.

      Exemptions

      Articles subject to 50 USC 1702(b), such as personal transfers of goods without value, donations, information or informational materials, and personal items transported during personal travel, were not subject to the fentanyl tariffs. 

      In an executive order signed on 11 April 2025, Trump exempted a range of electronic products and components from the reciprocal tariffs, including computers (including parts and accessories for their assembly), smartphones, flat panel displays, SSDs, computer monitors, various types of semiconductors, and integrated circuits. The exemptions are effective retroactively from 5 April. 

      Trump warned that the exemption will not be permanent, stating that semiconductors and the electronics supply chain will be looked at in the upcoming National Security Tariff Investigations. 

      The 10 percent fentanyl tariffs still applied to these exempted goods. 

      In addition, the following goods were not subject to the reciprocal tariffs: 

      • Articles subject to 50 USC 1702(b), such as personal transfers of goods without value, donations, information or informational materials, and personal items transported during personal travel;
      • Steel and aluminium articles and autos/auto parts that are already subject to the 25 percent Section 232 tariffs;
      • Copper, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, and lumber articles;
      • All articles that may become subject to future Section 232 tariffs;
      • Bullion; and
      • Energy and certain other minerals that are not available in the US. 

      On November 14, the US exempted a range of agricultural products that are not grown in the US from 13 November 2025 onward. According to the White House Fact Sheet, the products include:

      • Coffee and tea;
      • Tropical fruits and fruit juices;
      • Cocoa and spices;
      • Bananas, oranges, and tomatoes;
      • Beef; and
      • Additional fertilisers (some fertilisers have never been subject to the reciprocal tariffs).

      Any of these goods that originate in China were still subject to the 10 percent “fentanyl” tariff. Many are still subject to a 7.5 percent or higher Section 301 tariff.

      Temporary global tariff

      Shortly after the Supreme Court ruling, Trump signed a proclamation imposing a global 10 percent tariff on all countries under Section 122 of the Trade Act for 150 days starting on 24 February. The following day, he announced this would be raised to 15 percent, adding that in the coming months the administration would seek alternative tariffs to put in place in its stead. At the time of writing, his administration had not yet issued a formal order changing the rate to 15 percent, so the duty is currently levied at 10 percent.

      Section 122 of the Trade Act allows a president to impose tariffs of up to 15 percent for a maximum period of 150 days “to deal with large and serious United States balance-of-payments deficits”. Any extensions to this period must be approved by Congress.

      Exemptions

      According to the proclamation, the following items are exempt from the global tariff:

      • Certain critical minerals, metals used in currency and bullion, energy, and energy products;
      • Natural resources and fertilisers that cannot be grown, mined, or otherwise produced in the United States or grown, mined, or otherwise produced in sufficient quantities to meet domestic demand;
      • Certain agricultural products, including beef, tomatoes, and oranges;
      • Pharmaceuticals and pharmaceutical ingredients;
      • Certain electronics;
      • Passenger vehicles, certain light trucks, certain medium and heavy-duty vehicles, buses, and certain parts of passenger vehicles, light trucks, heavy-duty vehicles, and buses;
      • Certain aerospace products; and
      • Informational materials (e.g., books), donations, and accompanied baggage.

      Goods that are subject to the Section 232 tariffs are also exempt from the temporary global tariff.

      The full list of product exemptions can be found in Annex I and Annex II of the proclamation.

      Retaliatory action against Section 301 tariffs

      • 2 April 2018: Tariffs of 15% to 25% on 128 US products in retaliation for Section 232 tariffs.
      • 6 July 2018: 25% tariffs on US$34 billion worth of US goods in retaliation for List 1 tariffs.
      • 23 August 2018: 25% tariffs on US$16 billion of US goods in retaliation for List 1 tariffs.
      • 24 September 2018: 10% tariffs on US$60 billion of US goods in retaliation for List 3 tariffs.
      • 1 June 2019: Tariffs on US$60 billion in US goods raised to up to 25%.
      • 1 September 2019: Tariffs of 5% to 10% on US$75 billion in US goods in retaliation for List 4 tariffs.
      • 6 February 2020: Tariffs on some US goods were halved to 5% and 2.5% under the Phase One trade deal, effective 14 February.

       

      China began its tariff retaliation on 2 April 2018, following the US’s Section 301 tariffs. The first wave imposed duties of 15 percent to 25 percent on 128 US products, worth about US$3 billion. These included agricultural goods such as fruits, nuts, pork, and wine, aiming to target politically sensitive sectors in the US.

      On 6 July 2018, in direct response to the US imposing 25 percent tariffs on US$34 billion of Chinese imports (List 1), China levied its own 25 percent tariffs on an equal value of US goods. These included soybeans, automobiles, seafood, and other commodities. 

      In response to the US introducing a second batch of 25 percent tariffs on US$16 billion worth of Chinese goods (List 2) on 23 August 2018, China imposed a similar 25 percent tariff on 333 US products, which covered chemicals, medical devices, and petroleum-based products. 

      Following the List 3 tariffs imposed on 24 September 2018, China responded by imposing a 10 percent tariff on about US$60 billion of US goods. This wide-ranging list targeted thousands of products, including meat, LNG, and chemical goods. 

      In June 2019, after the US raised List 3 tariffs to 25 percent, China increased its retaliatory tariffs on US$60 billion in US goods, with rates ranging from 5 percent to 25 percent.  

      On 1 September 2019, China retaliated against new US tariffs (List 4A) by imposing additional 5 percent tariffs on US$75 billion of US exports. These included crude oil, aircraft parts, soybeans, and cars, expanding the scope of the trade conflict to cover nearly all US-China trade. 

      In January 2020, as part of the Phase One trade deal, China’s Finance Ministry announced that it would halve tariffs on 1,717 US goods, lowering the tariff on some items from 10 percent to 5 percent, and others from 5 percent to 2.5 percent, effective 14 February 2020. 

      Exemptions 

      Since 2019, China has granted exemptions to the retaliatory tariffs for certain American goods. The first two tariff exemption lists were released in September 2019, exempting 16 products in total, including many agricultural and fishery goods, such as shrimp and prawn seedlings, alfalfa meal, and whey for fodder. A further six products were added to this first batch of exemptions in December 2019. 

      In February 2020, China released a second batch of exemptions under two more lists covering 65 products, including timber, presswork, hydraulic motor, diaphragm pump, aircraft parts, and medical equipment like non-invasive ventilators and temperature sensors. 

      In the years since, China has continued to grant extensions to these exemption lists, usually one year in length. However, the last extension, granted in November 2024, expired on 28 February 2025, and has not been extended again, meaning all exemptions have now expired.

      According to a White House Fact Sheet released on 1 November 2025, following a meeting between Xi Jinping and Trump, “China will further extend the expiration of its market-based tariff exclusion process for imports from the United States and exclusions will remain valid until 31 December 2026“. This has yet to be confirmed by the Chinese side.

      On 19 February 2024, China retaliated against the US’s 10 percent tariff by imposing 15 percent tariffs on coal and LNG, and 10 percent tariffs on crude oil, machinery, and vehicles, along with export controls on rare earth metals.

      After the US raised fentanyl-related tariffs to 20 percent on 4 March 2025, China responded with tariffs of 15 percent on some agricultural goods and 10 percent on others, effective 10 March.

      On 10 April 2025, China imposed a 34 percent tariff on all US goods, matching the US tariff on China. 

      Following the US raising tariffs to 104 percent on 9 April, China increased its tariffs to 84 percent on 10 April, then to 125 percent on 12 April and announced it would not raise tariffs further. 

      On 12 May 2025, after diplomatic talks, China and the US agreed to reduce reciprocal tariffs to 10 percent for 90 days, with the US fentanyl tariff of 20 percent remaining. This reduced rate was extended for another 90 days in August, and then for one year on 30 October 2025, following a meeting between Trump and Xi, bringing the deadline forward to 10 November 2026.

      On 5 November 2025, China’s State Council Tariff Commission confirmed it was removing tariffs the 10 to 15 percent retaliatory tariffs on US agricultural products announced in March 2025 in response to the lowered fentanyl tariff rate. The tariffs were removed starting on 10 November 2025.

      Anti-dumping duties (POM copolymers)

      On 18 May 2025, China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) announced a 74.9 percent anti-dumping tariff on US companies exporting polyformaldehyde copolymer (POM copolymer) to China, following the conclusion of a year-long investigation. MOFCOM determined that US, EU, Taiwanese, and Japanese producers were dumping POM copolymers into the Chinese market at unfairly low prices, causing material injury to China’s domestic industry. The tariffs took effect on 19 May 2025, and remain in place for five years. 

      The tariffs apply specifically to products classified under Chinese customs codes 39071010 and 39071090, but other polymer variants like homopolymers or modified polyoxymethylene were not included in the investigation. 

      Importers must pay the anti-dumping duties based on the customs-assessed value of the goods, with import VAT levied on the total value, including tariffs and duties. Deposits collected during the preliminary period from 24 January to 18 May 2025, will be converted into final anti-dumping duties.
