US Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum (25% and 10%) affecting wind turbine and EV manufacturing costs Supply Chain Intelligence

How tariff-driven steel and aluminum price spikes are reshaping the cost structure of wind turbines and electric vehicles, forcing OEMs to re-engineer supply chains and pass through costs.

25% Steel Tariff10% Aluminum Tariff

Source: US International Trade Commission (USITC) & ESI Analysis

The US Section 232 tariffs impose a 25% tariff on steel and a 10% tariff on aluminum imports. For wind turbines, steel accounts for ~70-80% of the tower and nacelle weight, and aluminum is used in nacelle housings and heat exchangers. For EVs, steel and aluminum comprise ~50-60% of the vehicle body and chassis. These tariffs have increased the average cost of a wind turbine by $15-20/kW and an EV by $800-1,200 per vehicle, depending on supply chain sourcing. The tariffs have accelerated domestic investment in steel and aluminum production, but also created a two-tier market where US-made materials are priced at a premium, and imported materials face significant cost penalties. OEMs are responding by renegotiating contracts, increasing domestic content, and exploring tariff exclusions for critical components.

STRATEGIC VULNERABILITIES

Three Critical Chokepoints

Steel Plate & Sheet Supply

Wind turbine towers require heavy steel plates (ASTM A572 Grade 50) and hot-rolled coils. Domestic mills operate at ~85% capacity, and lead times for plate have extended to 12-16 weeks. Tariffs have reduced import competition, giving domestic mills pricing power. OEMs face a 25% cost premium on imported steel, but domestic steel is also priced higher due to reduced supply.

Aluminum Extrusions & Castings

EV battery enclosures and structural components rely on aluminum extrusions and high-pressure die castings. The 10% tariff on aluminum imports has increased costs for extrusions by 8-12%, as domestic smelters cannot meet demand. OEMs are forced to source from a limited number of US-based extruders, creating a bottleneck for EV production ramp-ups.

Tariff Exclusion Process

The exclusion process for Section 232 is complex and time-consuming, with approval rates around 30%. OEMs must prove that no US-produced alternative exists. This creates uncertainty and delays, forcing companies to either absorb costs or pass them to consumers. The process is a critical chokepoint for supply chain planning.

Executive Intelligence

ESI Analysis & Strategic Outlook

The Section 232 tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) constitute a structural cost shock and supply-chain distortion for US wind and EV manufacturing. Steel represents 70–80% of turbine tower and nacelle mass, while steel and aluminum account for 50–60% of EV body and chassis weight. The tariffs have added $15–20/kW to turbine costs and $800–1,200 per EV, directly compressing OEM margins and raising project capital expenditure. Critically, the tariffs have created a two-tier domestic market: US-produced steel and aluminum command a premium due to limited capacity and import substitution, while imported materials face punitive duties, forcing OEMs to either absorb costs or pass them downstream. This bifurcation introduces single-point-of-failure vulnerabilities—domestic mills are operating near capacity, and any disruption (labor strike, natural disaster) would cascade into severe supply shortages. Furthermore, the tariff regime has accelerated contract renegotiations, with OEMs seeking long-term fixed-price agreements and domestic content commitments, but the lack of exclusions for critical components (e.g., specialty alloys, forgings) leaves residual cost exposure. The systemic risk is amplified by the potential for retaliatory tariffs on US exports, which could erode the competitiveness of US-made turbines and EVs in global markets. Policy uncertainty—whether tariffs persist, escalate, or are replaced by quotas—creates a volatile planning environment, deterring capital investment in new manufacturing capacity. Strategic responses include reshoring supply chains, qualifying alternative materials, and lobbying for targeted exclusions, but these measures take years to yield cost relief. In the interim, the tariffs act as a regressive tax on the energy transition, increasing the levelized cost of wind and EV adoption, and potentially slowing the pace of decarbonization in the US.

Key Constraints

Critical Limitations & Risk Factors

  • Constraint 1: Domestic steel and aluminum production capacity is insufficient to meet the demand from wind and EV sectors, leading to reliance on imports subject to tariffs.
  • Constraint 2: The tariff exclusion process is bureaucratic and unpredictable, with approval rates around 30%, creating supply chain uncertainty.
  • Constraint 3: Substitution of steel with composites or aluminum with magnesium is technically feasible but requires significant R&D and retooling, limiting short-term feasibility.
  • Constraint 4: The tariffs have triggered retaliatory measures from trading partners, affecting US exports of wind turbines and EVs, and increasing costs for US manufacturers in global markets.
Frequently Asked Questions

Expert Answers on Supply Chain Intelligence

What specific steel and aluminum products used in wind turbines are most affected by Section 232 tariffs?
The most affected products include hot-rolled and cold-rolled steel plate used in tower sections, structural steel for nacelle frames, and aluminum extrusions for heat exchangers and nacelle housings. Additionally, specialty steel grades such as high-strength low-alloy (HSLA) steel for gearboxes and bearings are subject to the 25% tariff, though some exclusions exist for products not available domestically. The tariffs apply to raw steel and aluminum imports, but also to derivative products like fabricated steel components, which increases the cost of imported tower sections and subassemblies.
How do the Section 232 tariffs interact with the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) domestic content requirements for wind and EV tax credits?
The IRA offers a 10% bonus tax credit for wind projects and a $3,750–$7,500 EV tax credit if domestic content thresholds are met. However, Section 232 tariffs raise the cost of imported steel and aluminum, making it more expensive to meet domestic content requirements because US-made materials are priced at a premium. This creates a paradox: to qualify for IRA incentives, manufacturers must use more domestic steel/aluminum, but those materials are costlier due to tariffs, partially offsetting the tax credit benefit. Some OEMs are exploring 'safe harbor' provisions and negotiating with domestic mills for long-term pricing to mitigate this cost penalty.
What are the most common Section 232 tariff exclusions sought by wind and EV manufacturers, and what is the approval rate?
Common exclusions include specialty steel alloys (e.g., electrical steel for EV motors, corrosion-resistant steel for offshore wind towers) and aluminum products not produced in sufficient quantity or quality in the US. The Department of Commerce has approved exclusions for products where domestic availability is insufficient, but the process is lengthy (often 6–12 months) and approval rates have varied. As of 2024, roughly 70% of exclusion requests were approved, but many were for niche products. For high-volume commodities like standard steel plate, exclusions are rarely granted, forcing manufacturers to pay tariffs or source domestically at premium prices.
